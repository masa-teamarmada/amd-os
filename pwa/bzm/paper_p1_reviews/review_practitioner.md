# Practitioner referee report

**Manuscript:** "The Before Zero Model: measuring deep-tech ventures and their institutional nurseries before day zero"
**Referee background:** ~20 years directing a university technology-transfer office and gap fund; standing member of national seed-screening panels. I review as the practitioner referee; I leave the proof-checking to the theory referee and concentrate on whether the paper describes, and could survive contact with, the practice it theorizes.

---

## 1. Summary from the practitioner's chair

This is the rare theory paper written by people who have visibly sat in the meetings they model. The core structural insight — that venture evaluation and institutional evaluation are answers to two different questions ("what kills this deal?" vs. "what should we build next?"), and that the first is a conjunction while the second is a portfolio — is exactly right, and I have never seen it stated as cleanly as in Section 3: "an additive venture score hides the fatal zero; a multiplicative institution score turns one weak axis into a false death sentence." That sentence alone will circulate among TTO directors. The impossibility theorem, if it holds up, is genuinely useful to people like me: not as science but as a shield — a citable, National-Academies-grade reason to refuse the composite ranking that ministries and finance authorities demand every budget cycle. The E3 axiom ("unknown ≠ not started") corrects an abuse every mid-tier institution has suffered in national surveys. The comparative statics of Section 5 give policy designers a vocabulary (P-instruments vs. F-instruments vs. B-instruments vs. Π-instruments) that is better than anything currently in circulation.

And yet the paper repeatedly gets the *texture* of practice wrong in ways that will cost it its intended audience. It attacks a scoring practice partly of its own construction while ignoring the gated-additive designs that serious agencies already run; it does not engage a single deployed institutional-assessment instrument (HEInnovate — which also has eight dimensions — KEF, AUTM/ASTP benchmarking); its empirical section is constructed to be unfalsifiable and reads too clean; the single most consequential cut-point in the whole apparatus (the bylaws-gate threshold at $A_7 = 2$) has no published anchor; and several of its policy-lever mappings are naive or internally inconsistent about how instruments actually work. None of this is fatal. All of it is fixable. Major revision.

---

## 2. Verdict

**Major revision.** The theoretical core and the design recommendations deserve publication in Research Policy; the practice interface — positioning against real instruments, the calibration section's credibility, and the operational holes listed below — does not yet clear the bar. I want to see this paper again, and I say that about perhaps one submission in ten.

---

## 3. Major concerns

**MC1. The taxonomy of "composites found in practice" omits the dominant real-world design — which happens to be the paper's own recommendation.**
Section 4 asserts: "Every institution-adjusted score we have encountered in evaluation practice is one of these three" (pure multiplication, pure addition, general monotone blends). This is not my experience of practice, and Table 1 is missing the fourth family that dominates serious agencies: **additive scoring with per-criterion thresholds**. Horizon Europe's standard evaluation (three criteria, 0–5 each, threshold of 3 per criterion, 10 overall) *is* an additive score with a liveness guard. Japanese practice likewise gates rather than multiplies: platform-certification requirements in START-type programs, the restriction of 官民イノベーション出資 to designated universities — these are institutional *gates* on venture-ranked populations, i.e., precisely the "conditional gating" of Section 7. Two consequences. First, the opening vignette's "institution-adjusted project score: each seed's evaluated quality, multiplied by a coefficient" needs substantiation — the committee dynamics ring true (members absolutely do fight over weights, and the fight absolutely does assume a correct weighting exists), but the *multiplicative* instrument is the rarest form in the wild, and footnote 1's composite disclaimer does not discharge the burden of showing this practice exists as described. Second, and more importantly: the paper presents gated-additive shortlisting as what "survives the impossibility," as though it were a novel prescription — when it is close to the modal design of the world's largest funding program. The genuinely novel content is the *layer separation* (venture-liveness guard specifically, institution-gate specifically, never merged) and the theorem explaining *why* the incumbent gated designs are right. Reframe accordingly: the paper is stronger as "here is the theory that explains and sharpens what your best committees already grope toward" than as "here is what should replace what you do." Strawmen cost credibility with exactly the readers who could adopt this.

**MC2. Zero engagement with deployed institutional-readiness instruments.**
Section 2 claims "the merger itself has, to our knowledge, never been theorized" — perhaps true for the impossibility result, but the institutional ledger itself has operating cousins the paper never mentions: **HEInnovate** (EC/OECD self-assessment for entrepreneurial universities — *eight dimensions*, several overlapping the paper's axes), the UK's **Knowledge Exchange Framework** (which deliberately publishes a multi-perspective dashboard and *refuses* a single composite ranking — a live policy artifact already embodying Section 7's central recommendation), and AUTM/ASTP benchmarking surveys. A Research Policy referee pool will catch this; a practitioner audience will catch it faster. The absence matters in both directions: it overstates novelty (ERS is not the first eight-axis institutional rubric) and understates support (KEF's refusal to compositize is field evidence that the paper's recommendation is implementable — an agency did it). Locate ERS relative to HEInnovate explicitly: what does axiomatization add that a self-assessment wheel lacks? (Answer, I think: the aggregation license, the gate, the missing-data discipline, and the causal linkage to the venture ledger — that is a good answer; make it.)

**MC3. C3 is the load-bearing axiom, it is the one most likely to be false in this national context, and the main text hides the problem in a subordinate clause.**
The paper's own decision matrix (SM-B.7) concedes that if the Hansen J test rejects, "a direct institutional channel exists, C3 fails, and Theorem 3's premise is void regardless of the signatures." From my chair, the direct channel is obviously nonzero in Japan: the 東大発/imperial-university label moves investor term sheets, customer pilot decisions, and hiring at *fixed* technical fundamentals. The paper's only main-text acknowledgment is that C3 "is also, unusually for an axiom, empirically disciplined" — an elegant way of saying "our central premise might be false and everything downstream with it." Three requests. (i) Discuss the brand/halo channel in the main text, not in SM-B.7's parenthesis. (ii) Resolve the value-concept ambiguity that decides the question: is $f$ social/economic value or market price? A brand premium in private pricing at fixed $(R,S)$ fundamentals may be a transfer rather than value — in which case C3 survives the halo objection — but the paper never says which $f$ it means, and the axiom's plausibility hangs on it. (iii) State what survives *partial* C3 failure (small direct channel): does the impossibility degrade gracefully or collapse discontinuously? Practitioners will not stake governance redesign on a knife-edge axiom.

**MC4. Section 6 is constructed to be uncheckable, and it is too clean.**
Individually, each disclosure choice is defensible; jointly they leave nothing a reader can verify: composite cases with altered identifying details, *retrospective* regime assignments, calibration constants withheld (PF-010), momenta series definitions deposited elsewhere. The result: the one component a reader could in principle check against public data — the $\sigma_{SU}$ regime labels — is insulated by the compositing (Project T's "aligned" construction-decarbonization regime in 2012 strains my memory of that period; but with jittered dates and domains, no one can contest any label). Meanwhile the model never misfires: every founded-then-failed case reads NO_GO, every founded-then-progressing case reads GO, and the single case that fits poorly (Q, "decision-point rubric insufficient") is dropped — "retained for the survival panel only," a panel that **does not exist in this paper** (the text also cites "Table 2, bottom panel," which Table 2 does not have; drafting artifact, fix it). Retrospective + composite + one exclusion + zero disagreements is the signature of sanitized casework, and practitioners are the readers most sensitized to it. Remedies that would restore credibility: (a) report at least one case where the retrospective reading is wrong or ambiguous against outcome, or state explicitly that none was omitted; (b) disclose whether the studio's *actual* decisions were informed by precursor versions of this model — if yes, the "calibration organizes history" claim is partially circular (the model organizing decisions its authors' judgment produced) and must say so; (c) fix the T inconsistency (Table 2 reads "NO_GO (gate)"; the text says "more precisely, WAIT on the technology axis" — the formalism defines GO/not-GO plus $P\to0$ annihilation, so define WAIT vs. NO_GO before using a three-way vocabulary in the table, and while at it define "GO conditional on milestone" (row N), which the formalism nowhere licenses).

**MC5. The bylaws gate: right diagnosis, unanchored threshold, and a sequencing prescription that practice contradicts.**
The gate is the most Japan-true element of the paper — I have watched an institution with a funded incubator and a gap fund lose eighteen months per deal for want of an equity policy, and "an institution scoring 44% on capabilities but with no legal pathway for a professor to found a company has idle capacity, not readiness" is exactly right *as diagnosis*. Three problems with the implementation. (i) **The operative threshold has no anchor.** Proposition 1 sets $g(a)=1$ for $a \ge L$, default $L=2$; SM-D publishes level-0 and level-4 anchors only ("illustrative"), and SM-A concedes $g(1)=0.25$ is "fixed by rubric-design convention... not derived" [GAP]. The single most consequential cut-point in the whole system — the difference between ERS = 0 and ERS = full — sits at a rubric level whose anchor the paper does not state. Publish the level-1, level-2, level-3 anchor sheet for axis 7 in SM-D, full stop. (ii) **The level-0 anchor conflates two different states.** "No rules; each case improvised" scored 0 (total annihilation) contradicts observed reality: institutions without formal bylaws demonstrably produce spin-outs — slowly, painfully, with founder-borne friction — and historically the first deals *precede* the rules almost everywhere (the rules get written because of the first deals). "No rules and no de-facto pathway" and "no rules but exercised improvisation" are different states; the second is common, productive, and should not gate to zero. (iii) **"Bylaws before showcases" (§7) is naive as universal sequencing.** Bylaws written by administrators with zero deal experience are, in my direct observation, the ones that later block deals — the Japanese pathology of premature equity policies demanding excessive founder dilution is exactly this. Best practice is iterated deal–rule co-evolution, not rules-first. The gate can survive all three fixes; the prescription cannot survive unmodified.

**MC6. E3's renormalization is strategically gameable, and the paper does not notice.**
SM-A Step 4: "an axis with $A_k=\bot$ leaves both numerator and denominator." Then an institution whose incubation program would honestly score 1 *raises its ERS by declining to evidence the axis* — unknown is dropped from the denominator, weakness is not. The moment ERS carries funding or reputational stakes (which Section 7 proposes), strategic non-disclosure becomes the dominant move, and E3 — designed as an honesty device — becomes an opacity subsidy. The fix practitioners would insist on: distinguish "assessor could not observe" from "institution declined to evidence," and code refusal at worst-case (as credit-rating practice does), with $\bot$ reserved for genuine non-observability. Relatedly: the $(\mathrm{ERS}, K_{obs})$ pair is honest, but agencies *will* strip the $K_{obs}$ and rank the scalar — scores with different observed sets are not on a common scale, and the paper should say what an agency may and may not do with heterogeneous-$K_{obs}$ portfolios.

**MC7. The policy-lever mapping is one-to-one in rhetoric, many-to-many in reality — and the paper contradicts itself about its flagship instrument.**
Section 7: "gap funds and fellowships raise F." Section 5, four paragraphs earlier: "Higher ERS shifts the drift of R (faster realization under better gap funding and mentoring)." Both are true, which is the problem: a gap fund is a *bundle* — slack (F) plus purchased maturation (R-drift) — and every practitioner designs it as such (we milestone the tranches precisely to force the money to become R rather than runway). The clean instrument taxonomy of Proposition 2 is the paper's most policy-useful artifact; it will be *more* useful, not less, if it admits that real instruments load on multiple arguments and that instrument *design* chooses the loading. Same for "subsidies... raise P": a demand commitment raises P; a generic founding subsidy raises F or lowers B; a production subsidy raises unit-economics viability, which the paper folds into P — revealing, incidentally, that P is doing double duty (addressable scale *and* unit-economics viability are different diligence objects, and Project Y's "$P \to 0$" is a margin-viability zero, not a scale zero; sharpen the construct or split it).

**MC8. "Credible policy commitment is a founding subsidy obtained without spending a yen" is the paper's most quotable line and its most naive.**
The comparative static ($\partial\theta^*/\partial\pi_{kk} < 0$) is sound and genuinely valuable — I would use it tomorrow to argue for multi-year program structures. But credibility is not free and is not a dial: it is bought with fiscal commitment devices (multi-year appropriations against Japan's 単年度予算主義 single-year budget principle, statutory mechanisms like GX bonds), and it is constrained by time inconsistency — founders discount announcements precisely because governments cannot bind successors, so $\pi_{kk}$ is an equilibrium belief object, not a policy instrument. The correct claim is conditional: *if* credibility can be manufactured, it is the cheapest lever per yen transferred — and the cost of manufacturing it is the hard part the model does not price. One sentence of humility here saves the result.

**MC9. The 24-month outcome window contradicts the paper's own premise, and the registry's power is years away.**
Section 1: value accumulates over "typically five to fifteen" years. Section 7: the registry scores readings "on twenty-four-month outcome classes." In drug discovery or advanced materials, "stagnant at 24 months" is frequently the *normal trajectory of a good project* — the window will misclassify slow-good ventures systematically, in exactly the domains the paper claims for its scope. Either defend 24 months as an early-trajectory classifier with domain-specific class definitions (and show the drug-discovery mapping flagged as "provisional" in Table 2's notes), or lengthen it. Relatedly, be honest about power: Simpson-reversal and Hausman tests on institution coefficients within venture quartiles need portfolio-scale N spanning many institutions; the studio pipeline can never power them, and national-registry participation is a prerequisite the program should state as such. Also resolve the outcome-taxonomy asymmetry a practitioner will spot instantly: Project T is "stagnant (zombie)" *because* it survives on public tailwind, while Project R is "progressing (public grant)" — grant-sustained survival is zombie fuel in one row and progress in another. The class definitions must distinguish milestone/revenue progress from subsidy persistence, in the text and not only in a deposited protocol.

**MC10. The single-agent founding decision assumes away the practitioner's actual job.**
SM-C.0: the founder–institution pair is "modeled as a single joint-value-maximizing agent." The pre-founding stage's central *practical* difficulty is that the parties are not a single agent: the professor's threshold (one more paper, one more grant) differs from the university's (licensing revenue vs. equity risk, COI exposure) differs from the investor's (deploy now). Most of my working hours are spent on exactly the divergence this assumption deletes — equity splits, IP assignment vs. license, leave policies. A first-pass model may legitimately assume it away, but the main text never flags it as a limitation (Section 5's scope note lists regime observability, not agency). One paragraph acknowledging that GO is the *aligned-pair* benchmark, and that divergent stakeholder thresholds around $\theta^*$ are the natural next model, would inoculate the paper against its most obvious practitioner objection.

## 4. Practice errors and naive claims (rapid fire)

- **"Axioms that any technology-transfer practitioner would endorse" (Theorem 1) is oversold.** I endorse E3 and E4 without reservation, and "networks do substitute for in-house capacity" (E2) is real and well said. But E1/E2-s, as *value* claims, cut against practitioner belief in complementarities: a gap fund without scouting funds junk; an EIR pool without deal flow idles; going 0→1 on your weakest axis is worth more than 3→4 on your strongest. That is $\rho < 1$ in the CES family the authors themselves park in SM-A Remark (iii), not the $\rho = 1$ the theorem enthrones. The uniqueness result is driven by E2-s — a *rubric design convention* (equal intervals), not a fact about institutions. Present Theorem 1 as "the unique aggregate consistent with an equal-interval reporting convention," which is true, rather than as practice consensus, which is not.
- **Axes 2, 4, 5, 8 are often one office.** At mid-tier Japanese universities the TLO, industry-collaboration, gap-fund, and government-program functions are three people in the 産学連携本部. Scoring them as four independent axes measures the same capability four times; assessment independence (the paper's defense of E1) is weakest exactly where the substitution story is told. Discuss correlated measurement.
- **Missing axis: leadership commitment and faculty incentives.** After bylaws, the blocker I see most is career penalty — founding hurts promotion at most national universities. This is not axis 7 (legal pathway ≠ incentive design) and lives nowhere in the rubric. If it was excluded because it resists document-anchored observation, say so.
- **σ_SU invites domain-boundary gaming, unaddressed.** Every proposal team defines its domain to capture the adjacent hot policy budget ("AI × green × health"); momenta are computed *per domain*, so the advocate chooses the denominator. Conveniently, both pending projects (M, N) sit at "$S_1 \to S_2$." Who fixes domain boundaries, and how contestable are they? This is the most gameable object in the system and the paper is silent.
- **Withheld calibration constants are incompatible with public deployment.** PF-010 may protect the studio's operating data, but no procurement-fair agency can adopt an instrument whose constants are embargoed. The paper wants to be both a public measurement standard and a proprietary asset; pick one per layer (theorems and rubrics public; the studio's *fitted values* proprietary is a defensible split — the current text blurs it).
- **"Retire the institution-adjusted single ranking" ignores why the ranking exists.** The composite is an accountability artifact: it gives program officers an audit trail against 会計検査院 and parliamentary questions, and gives ministries a budget-negotiation exhibit. Two ledgers plus deliberation reads as *more discretion*, which is precisely what bureaucracies fear. The paper's own devices are the answer — gates are *more* auditable than continuous coefficients ("we fund only institutions with exercised bylaws" survives an audit better than any weighting) — but the paper never makes the audit-defensibility argument, which is the one that wins inside an agency.
- **The sharpest policy implication is missing: incorporation-deadline subsidies are negative timing instruments.** Programs that condition grants on incorporation within the program period *manufacture premature GO* — founding at $\sigma < \theta^*$ by construction. This is the single most common way real zombies get made (and, I suspect, part of Project T's true story). Theorem 4 gives the formal language for the field's most damaging instrument design error, and the paper walks past it with one sentence about "timing support." Write the paragraph; practitioners will quote it for a decade.
- **Measurement capacity is endogenous.** Scoring P requires unit-economics diligence — the capacity that axis $A_1$ measures. Institutions with low $A_1$ cannot score PRS reliably, so venture-ledger quality is itself a function of the ERS of whoever scores it. Worth a sentence; it also strengthens the case for studio/agency-level scoring rather than self-assessment.
- **No inter-rater reliability, no scoring cost, no assessor qualification.** The first three questions any adopting TTO director asks. Even a modest statement (two raters, n = 8, agreement statistics; hours per institution) would do more for adoption than SM-B does.

## 5. What would make practitioners cite and use this paper

1. **Position against the instruments we already run.** One subsection: ERS vs. HEInnovate vs. KEF vs. AUTM/ASTP metrics; Table 1 extended with the gated-additive family (Horizon per-criterion thresholds) and Japanese institutional gates. The honest framing — "your best committees already build gated systems; here is the theorem for why, and here is what they still get wrong (layer merging, missing-data imputation, unanchored gates)" — converts the paper from an attack into ammunition.
2. **Publish the full anchor sheets**, especially axis 7 levels 1–3 (the gate threshold), in SM-D. An unanchored gate is an unusable gate. Add the refusal-vs-unobservable coding rule (MC6) and a short anti-gaming annex (K_obs, bylaws theater, domain boundaries).
3. **Main-text treatment of the brand channel and partial-C3 failure** (MC3). Practitioners will not redesign governance on an axiom whose failure mode is total.
4. **Make M and N the registry's first pre-registered entries, explicitly.** The paper's honesty discipline is its best feature; putting the authors' own two pending readings on the public record *before* outcomes — named as predictions #1 and #2 — is the costly signal that would make me believe the rest.
5. **Add the incorporation-deadline paragraph** (negative timing instruments) and the audit-defensibility argument for gates. Those are the two passages a TTO director would photocopy for the ministry meeting.
6. **One admitted misfire in Section 6**, or an explicit statement that none exists and why; plus the circularity disclosure (were studio decisions made with precursor versions of this model?).
7. **A one-page translation of Section 7 for agencies**: when the ministry demands the composite ranking, here is the shortlist template you hand them instead (additive with published $(\alpha, \beta)$, venture-liveness guard, institutional gate, two-ledger budget split). The paper contains all the pieces; assemble them where a program officer can find them.

The bones of this paper are excellent. It knows what the two meetings are — the deal meeting and the capacity meeting — and it has proven why they must remain two meetings. It now needs to demonstrate the same fluency about the world outside the meeting room: the audit trail, the gaming, the instruments already deployed, and the cases that don't fit. Major revision, with genuine enthusiasm.
