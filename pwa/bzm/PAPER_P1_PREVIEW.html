Abstract: Deep-tech ventures born from universities accumulate — or fail to accumulate — most of their value before incorporation, yet this “Before Zero” stage has no measurement theory. Evaluation practice fills the vacuum with institution-adjusted single scores — a project’s rating adjusted by its host institution’s capabilities — and committees debate the correct weighting. We prove the debate has no answer. Under four requirements any committee member would separately endorse (a dead venture is worth zero anywhere; a better host never hurts; hosts act on value through what they do to the venture; value moves continuously) together with two facts of technology-transfer practice (institutional capabilities bind at different venture stages, and act over the venture’s remaining journey), no monotone single score merging venture and institution can represent venture value — not the current weighting, not any weighting: an impossibility in the Arrow mold. Fields that rank and fund by such scores anyway leave statistical scars, which we derive and pre-register — including a Simpson reversal manufactured by score-based selection itself. What survives is a complete working design: a multiplicative venture ledger (PRS\mathrm{PRS} = potential × realization × survival), an additive institutional ledger (ERS\mathrm{ERS}, proven the unique admissible aggregate), a shared Triple-Helix macro state, and a founding-timing rule that reads both ledgers without merging them. Conditional gating — the design the field’s most serious agencies already run — is thereby proven right rather than merely customary. A retrospective calibration on eight deep-tech projects (2007–2026) illustrates the system, and a registered falsification program, Before Zero Studies, states in advance the evidence that would prove it wrong. Keywords: deep-tech ventures; university spin-outs; readiness measurement; technology readiness levels; Triple Helix; impossibility theorem; real options; research commercialization

1. Introduction

A national screening panel convenes to rank some forty university deep-tech seeds competing for a commercialization fund.¹ Its instrument is an “institution-adjusted project score”: each seed’s evaluated quality, multiplied by a coefficient reflecting its host university’s technology-transfer capabilities. Midway through the session a member proposes re-weighting the institutional coefficient; two seeds swap ranks; a debate follows about which weighting is correct — a debate that assumes its own answer: that some weighting is, that the committee’s task is calibration. This paper proves the assumption false. Under requirements every member of that committee would separately endorse — a dead venture is worth zero anywhere; a better institution never hurts — together with two facts of technology-transfer practice — institutional capabilities bind at different venture stages, and act over the venture’s remaining journey — no institution-adjusted single score exists: not the current weighting, not the proposed one, not any monotone combination however sophisticated. The committee is not miscalibrating an instrument; it is calibrating a nonexistent one.

The setting is the pre-founding stage of deep-tech venturing, which we call Before Zero: the years — typically five to fifteen — during which a research result inside a university or national laboratory matures toward incorporation (“day zero”). Along that road the decisive assets accumulate — technology readiness, intellectual property, regulatory position, founding team, institutional scaffolding (Shane, 2004; Grimaldi et al., 2011; Perkmann et al., 2013) — so that by day zero much of a deep-tech venture’s fate is already committed. Yet the stage is a measurement vacuum: too early for valuation, it is governed by readiness vocabularies (Mankins, 1995) and scorecards, and increasingly by policy demands for composite rankings of seeds, institutions, and ecosystems (Nardo et al., 2008).

This paper proposes a measurement theory for that vacuum: the Before Zero Model (BZM). Its architecture is deliberately dual. The venture ledger scores each project multiplicatively, PRS=P⋅R⋅S\mathrm{PRS} = P \cdot R \cdot S (potential scale × realization readiness × survival probability), because a pre-founding venture is a conjunction of necessary conditions in which any zero is fatal. The institutional ledger scores the hosting nursery additively, ERS\mathrm{ERS}, a weighted sum over eight rubric-scored capability axes, because an institution is a portfolio of partially substitutable capabilities whose gaps must stay visible. A shared macro state σSU\sigma_{SU} — the shifted geometric mean of academic, industrial, and governmental momenta, Triple Helix in conception (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000) — drives the venture ledger’s dynamics.

The central result explains why the architecture must be dual. We define the institution-adjusted value of a seed honestly — the expected discounted value of the venture under its institution’s nurturing dynamics — so that a dead venture is worth zero anywhere, a better nursery never hurts, institutions act on value through what they do to the venture, and value moves continuously. Theorem 3 establishes that once institutional capabilities are stage-contingent — scouting and incubation bind early, licensing and the corporate interface bind late — no single-score merger of the two ledgers, monotone in its indices, can represent that value: the true comparison of two nurseries reverses across venture strata, and one index cannot lie on both sides of a reversal. Corollary 3.1 converts the impossibility into empirics: a field that ranks — and therefore funds, and therefore samples — by single scores leaves statistical scars, registered as diagnostics with error rates fixed in advance — including a Simpson reversal manufactured by score-selected funding itself.

The result reads plainly. When a committee debates which institutional weighting is correct, the debate has no answer: every weighting is right for ventures at one stage of the journey and provably wrong for ventures at another, because what a nursery does for a seed depends on where the seed stands. What the theorem forbids is not evaluation but compression. What survives is everything a working committee actually needs: an additive shortlist whose weights are published as the political choices they are, guarded so that no dead venture outranks a live one; conditional gates — rank ventures on their own merits, fund them only at institutions that clear stated thresholds — which Section 4 proves correct rather than merely customary; and a governance split in which project money follows the venture ledger and capacity-building money follows the institutional ledger. A committee that already gates has been right all along; this paper supplies the reason, and Section 7 the audit answer for the ministry that demands the composite anyway.

Two further results complete the system. Theorem 1 shows the weighted sum is the unique institutional aggregate — in rubric units, under five practice-grounded axioms and the rubric’s equal-interval reporting convention — by additive conjoint measurement (Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky, 1971), with a precondition gate for formal bylaws (Proposition 1) in the spirit of Alkire and Foster (2011). Theorem 4 shows the system is operable: the go/wait decision is formalized as an optimal-stopping rule in the regime-switching real-options tradition (Dixit and Pindyck, 1994; Guo, Miao and Morellec, 2005), with a threshold derived from project economics and — distinctively — monitored on a state variable constructed by the auditable measurement layer of Section 3. We order the contributions deliberately: measurement first; the decision operator demonstrates completeness rather than headlining the paper.

We exercise the system on the operating records of a single venture studio: eight deep-tech projects across Japanese universities and national laboratories, 2007–2026, reported as composites with type-named institutions. The exercise is retrospective calibration under explicit Tier discipline — outcomes were known when rubrics were scored, so it demonstrates organization of history, not prediction. Its two vignettes are instructive: a macro-blessed, technology-hollow project any composite would have founded (history did, into a decade of stagnation), and a technically presentable project whose potential-scale zero the multiplicative ledger catches while additive scores rank it above the first.

Three contributions, then, to this journal’s readership. A measurement theory for an unmeasured stage: axiomatic foundations for evaluating ventures that do not yet exist, where valuation is structurally silent. An impossibility as policy guardrail: proof that the single-score instrument evaluation practice keeps demanding cannot exist, together with what legitimately survives. A falsification-registered research program — Before Zero Studies — whose central claims are built to be testable and whose violation signatures are specified in advance (Popper, 1959).

2. Two evaluation problems, one field

Readiness scales are the pre-founding evaluator’s incumbent technology. Technology Readiness Levels, born as a NASA communication protocol for hardware maturity (Mankins, 1995), have diffused into mandates across European framework programs and national funding agencies, multiplying into business, societal, and organizational variants. Their strength is real: a shared ordinal ladder disciplines conversation across communities. Their limits are equally documented (Olechowski et al., 2020): each scale is a single ordinal axis with no aggregation theory — nothing says how technology maturity should combine with market, team, or institutional maturity into an assessment of the venture, and nothing prevents practitioners from averaging ordinal levels as if they were cardinal. Readiness scales, in short, supply vocabulary without measurement theory.

Where multiple axes must become one number, practice reaches for composite indicators, whose methodological literature has long sounded alarms: the canonical handbook catalogs the arbitrariness of weighting and normalization choices (Nardo et al., 2008); systematic reviews document rank reversals under defensible alternative constructions (Greco et al., 2019); and the deepest critique is Sen’s (1977) — an index that buries heterogeneous dimensions in a single number conceals precisely the trade-offs that deliberation should surface. The constructive exception: the Alkire–Foster poverty measures (Alkire and Foster, 2011) survive scrutiny by axiomatizing identification separately from aggregation. Innovation evaluation has imported the composite habit without importing the axiomatic discipline; this paper’s Proposition 1 and Theorem 3 are, in one reading, that discipline arriving.

Nor is the demand hypothetical: the merger is load-bearing inside the world’s largest evaluation systems. The U.S. National Institutes of Health long scored five review criteria — among them the host environment — and merged them into the single Overall Impact score that orders its funding; its simplified review framework, in force since January 2025, removed the numerical score from precisely the expertise-and-resources factor, converting it to a sufficiency judgment — a system-scale move toward the gate structure this paper derives [verify at S6: NIH NOT-OD-24-010]. The United Kingdom operates both sides of the question: the Research Excellence Framework merges an institutional environment profile, at a stated weight, into the single quality profile that allocates public research funding, while its knowledge-exchange counterpart, the KEF, deliberately publishes perspective dashboards and refuses any composite ranking [verify at S6]. National innovation scoreboards compress whole systems into single indices whose fragility is documented in this journal (Grupp and Mogee, 2004; Grupp and Schubert, 2010). The merger this paper studies is the modal instrument — and where practice has begun abandoning it, it has done so by instinct, without the theorem that says the instinct is sound.

A third literature studies our institutional layer directly: technology-transfer offices, gap funds, incubators, entrepreneurs-in-residence, and spin-out policies (Bozeman, 2000; Shane, 2004; Grimaldi et al., 2011; Munari et al., 2016; Hayter et al., 2018), whose collective finding is that institutional capabilities materially shape spin-out formation and survival. Those capabilities serve the institutional face of the valley of death (Auerswald and Branscomb, 2003), and its stage-wise crossing — critical junctures at which different resources bind (Vohora, Wright and Lockett, 2004) — is precisely the stage-contingency Section 4 turns into an impossibility. Yet this literature measures the institution separately from the venture, while evaluation practice merges the two levels daily — and the merger itself has, to our knowledge, never been theorized. Deployed instruments exist at the institutional layer — HEInnovate, the KEF dashboards, the AUTM and ASTP surveys [verify at S6] — and Section 7 positions our ledger against each; none carries an aggregation theory, and none theorizes the cross-level merger. The gap this paper fills is the aggregation across levels that practice performs without a license.

The Triple Helix program (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000) supplies our macro layer’s conceptual frame — innovation capacity emerges from the co-evolution of academia, industry, and government — made quantitative in mutual-information synergy indicators (Leydesdorff, 2003). What it lacks is a venture-facing state variable — a quantity a founder–institution pair can observe and a stopping rule can condition on: TAIGT_{AIG} diagnoses a national or regional system, and no decision about a particular seed follows from it. Our σSU\sigma_{SU} is built for exactly that vacant slot, and Section 3 keeps the two objects distinct.

Entry timing under uncertainty is the fifth ingredient, supplied in mature form by the real-options literature: irreversible investment, option value of waiting, threshold policies (McDonald and Siegel, 1986; Dixit and Pindyck, 1994), staged innovation investment (McGrath, 1997), regime-switching thresholds, financing-state effects, policy uncertainty, and stopping under incomplete information (Guo and Zhang, 2004; Guo, Miao and Morellec, 2005; Jobert and Rogers, 2006; Hassett and Metcalf, 1999; Boyle and Guthrie, 2003; Décamps, Mariotti and Villeneuve, 2005). One feature fails before day zero: the monitored state is a firm-level cash flow or output price — nonexistent for pre-founding ventures — so the threshold floats free of any measurement system. Section 5 marries that stopping machinery to Section 3’s measurement layer — the first such marriage in this domain, we believe; the marriage, not the machinery, is the claim.

Finally, the tools of this paper’s central result come from a tradition innovation studies has rarely visited: the measurement-theoretic and social-choice lineage. Arrow (1963) established the template — reasonable axioms, jointly unsatisfiable — and the conjoint-measurement school (Debreu, 1960; Luce and Tukey, 1964; Krantz et al., 1971; Wakker, 1989) characterized exactly when multi-attribute orderings admit additive or separable representations. These results are sixty years old, rigorous, and almost entirely absent from the evaluation practices they indict. Importing them is the methodological move: Theorem 1 applies conjoint measurement to an institutional object those authors never considered, and Section 4 rebuilds Arrow’s template at the venture–institution interface, richness supplied not by preference profiles but by how nurseries act on seeds.

The synthesis: five literatures each hold one piece — vocabulary, aggregation warnings, the institutional layer, the macro frame, timing — and none the connective theory: one that respects venture multiplicativity, derives institutional additivity, proves the layers unmergeable, and hands the result to a stopping rule. The Before Zero Model is built to be that theory — and built so its central claims can be wrong in public, axioms stated for inspection, violation signatures registered for detection.

3. The two-layer observation system

A research seed ii at institution jj, before incorporation, offers no cash flows, market shares, or churn — only rubric-scored states: technology maturity and its preparations, the host’s capabilities, and the attention of academia, industry, and government to its domain. Organizing these observables into decision support without false precision is the Before Zero measurement problem; our answer is two ledgers of different mathematical shape plus one shared macro state.

The venture ledger assigns each project the triple (P,R,S)(P, R, S) and the score PRSi(t)=Pi(t)⋅Ri(t)⋅Si(t),\mathrm{PRS}_{i}(t) = P_i(t) \cdot R_i(t) \cdot S_i(t), where PP is the potential scale conditional on full realization, RR is realization readiness, the progress of technology, team, and commercialization preparations toward it, and SS is the non-financing survival probability of completing the journey (funding exhaustion is priced once, at Section 5’s runway barrier). The form is not stylistic: a pre-founding venture is a conjunction of necessary conditions, a weakest-link economy in the O-ring sense (Kremer, 1993) where excellence in two factors cannot rescue a zero in the third; a seed with world-class science but a market of zero economic size is not “average” but dead — a mortality that multiplication, unlike any averaging rule, encodes.

Practice runs two diligence objects together as “market size.” PP is attainable-unit-economics-priced addressable scale, the opportunity’s value ceiling conditional on full realization, priced at physically attainable performance and cost: a market-size zero (no demand at any price) and a margin-viability zero (each unit sold destroys value) are both PP-zeros. The rubric anchors keep the two sub-objects distinct — one cannot be quoted for the other — and Project Y’s P→0P \to 0 in Section 6 is a viability zero, not a size zero.

All readings are five-level ordinal rubrics anchored by observable states, taken at the decision date, under three disciplines — dated information sets, endogenous measurement capacity (hence agency- or studio-level scoring), and ex-ante-fixed domain boundaries (SM-D.3, SM-D.4). Disclosure is a declared layer split: theorems, rubrics, anchor logic, and coding conventions are public; fitted calibration constants are proprietary, reported as procedure; everything an adopter needs to rescore from its own records is public (SM-D.4).

The ledgers share one macro state. With μA\mu_A, μI\mu_I, and μG\mu_G the momenta of academic, industrial, and governmental attention to the seed’s domain, define σSU(t)=(μA+1)(μI+1)(μG+1)3−1.\sigma_{SU}(t) = \sqrt[3]{(\mu_A + 1)(\mu_I + 1)(\mu_G + 1)} - 1. This is Triple Helix in conception (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000): the geometric-mean form, an equal-weight Cobb–Douglas aggregate (Cobb and Douglas, 1928), makes tailwind a joint property of the three helices. σSU\sigma_{SU} is not Leydesdorff’s (2003) mutual-information indicator TAIGT_{AIG} — a system-level synergy diagnostic rather than a venture-facing state variable. It evolves through regimes (dormant S0S_0, warming S1S_1, aligned S2S_2) on which the venture ledger’s dynamics — the drift of RR, the hazard behind SS — condition.

The institutional ledger observes the nursery rather than the seed: institution jj is scored on eight axes A1,…,A8A_1, \ldots, A_8 (0–4 rubrics; SM-D) — seed scouting and technology assessment; IP and licensing; incubation and entrepreneurship support; industry-collaboration interface; funding access, including gap funds; entrepreneurial management talent; entrepreneurship bylaws and governance; and government and policy linkage. Five axioms discipline aggregation. E1 (separability): each axis’s contribution is assessable without knowing the others. E2 (compensability): observed capabilities partially substitute; only the bylaws precondition (Proposition 1) is individually fatal. E3 (audited missing-visibility): “unknown” and “not started” are different states (Rubin, 1976), and declining to evidence an axis scores the floor, so opacity cannot raise a score (SM-A.5). E4 (monotonicity): improving any observed axis raises readiness; no axis is inert. E5 (cross-context invariance): trade-off rates between observed capabilities do not depend on which further axes are observed. Each axiom is a statement of technology-transfer practice before it is mathematics (practice readings: SM-A.1).

One qualification sharpens the additive form. Proposition 1 (precondition gate). Axis 7 — entrepreneurship bylaws and formal rules — enters not as an additive term but as a gate: ERSj=100⋅g(Aj7)⋅F(Aj,−7)\mathrm{ERS}_j = 100 \cdot g(A_{j7}) \cdot F(A_{j,-7}), where gg is a step (or steep sigmoid) function and FF is the Theorem-1 aggregate over the remaining axes. An institution scoring 44% on capabilities but with no legal pathway for a professor to found a company has idle capacity, not readiness. The gate echoes Alkire and Foster’s (2011) identification-then-aggregation discipline; E2 is withdrawn for axis 7 alone, so the gate refines rather than contradicts Theorem 1 (SM-A).

One merger is prohibited outright. Theorem 2 (no multiplicative cross-combination; proof in SM-A). Any multiplicative cross-combination ERSα⋅PRS1−α\mathrm{ERS}^{\alpha} \cdot \mathrm{PRS}^{1-\alpha}, α∈(0,1)\alpha \in (0,1), preserves the institutional ordering at each fixed venture state — but destroys the calibrated reporting scale of the institutional ledger: rubric steps no longer trade at level-independent rates within the institutional layer, and the marginal credit for every institutional axis becomes contingent on the venture state, so the nursery is paid once inside the venture score and again as a multiplier. What the merger breaks is the interval-scale report by which committees audit published weights; the venture-contingent credit is double counting, whose value-level form Section 4 proves. It disqualifies the most popular composite as a reporting instrument; Section 4 asks whether any single score is possible.

The shape difference’s economic logic drives everything downstream. A venture is a conjunction: every necessary condition must hold, so the informative question is “what kills this deal?”, and the multiplicative ledger makes the killer visible as the smallest factor. An institution is a portfolio: capabilities substitute at the margin, so the informative question is “what should we build next?”, and the additive ledger makes the answer visible as the largest weighted gap. Forcing either object into the other’s shape destroys the information the evaluator needs: an additive venture score hides the fatal zero; a multiplicative institution score turns one weak axis into a false death sentence.

4. The impossibility theorem

The temptation this section formalizes is ubiquitous: a committee with both ledgers’ scores wants one number per (project, institution) pair, f(P,R,S;A)∈ℝ≥0f(P, R, S; A) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}, an “institution-adjusted project value” for ranking a national portfolio. We take the demand seriously and prove its target empty: admit two observable facts about how nurseries act on seeds, and no monotone merger of the ledgers represents venture value.

Honestly defined, a seed is worth what it will become under its institution’s care, so f(P,R,S;A)f(P, R, S; A) is the value function of a nurturing environment: ff is the expected discounted payoff under laws of motion whose coefficients depend on AA (SM-B’s admissible class). Three requirements become structural properties. C1′ (venture annihilation): a venture with no market or no survival (min⁡(P,S)=0\min(P, S) = 0) is absorbed at zero regardless of AA: nobody funds a dead project because its university has excellent bylaws. Realization is deliberately excluded: R=0R = 0 is an early seed’s normal state — an option gap funds exist to fund, not a corpse. C2′ (institutional monotonicity): a better nursery never makes a live project less valuable — improving any axis improves drifts and hazards, hence value. C4′ (continuity): value moves continuously in states and profiles. The defining restriction is C3′ (causal channel): institutional axes enter only the venture’s laws of motion (realization, survival, slack, exposure) and carry no direct premium at a fixed venture path; bylaws, gap funds, and mentors work by accelerating realization, improving survival, and buying runway. Section 7’s Hansen-GMM overidentification test targets exactly this excluded residual (Hansen, 1982): registered, not dogma.

The restriction must face the certification evidence: prominent affiliations raise survival and speed capital acquisition (Stuart, Hoang and Hybels, 1999), entrepreneurs pay for reputable backing (Hsu, 2004), early-stage public grants causally improve subsequent private financing (Howell, 2017). But certification demonstrably moves resource access, trajectory consequences inside the licensed carriers; the class excludes only the residual halo at fixed trajectory, exactly what the Section 7 test estimates. And Theorem 3 relies only on the observable reversal SH: a documented direct channel would reinterpret the class, not rescue the single score (SM-B.8).

SH (stage-contingent operability): capabilities bind at different stages of the journey — scouting and incubation early, licensing execution and the corporate interface late (Vohora, Wright and Lockett, 2004) — so the ranking of two institutional profiles can genuinely reverse across venture strata. ED (exposure-duration asymmetry): institutions act over the venture’s remaining journey, compounding strongly for an early seed and weakly for one nearly done, so the ranking of two ventures can reverse as their shared institution strengthens (SM-B.3 exhibits both in closed form). Both are observable, standard technology-transfer talk, and enter the registered program as testable premises.

A lemma disposes of a class unused in practice: composites Φ(g(P,R,S),h(A))\Phi(g(P,R,S), h(A)) strictly increasing in both arguments die at the death set min⁡(P,S)=0\min(P,S)=0, where every institutional comparison ties at zero (Lemma SM-B.2). Fielded composites are weakly monotone — flat in the institution where the venture is dead — so the question is whether weakly monotone mergers survive SH and ED.

Theorem 3 (single-index impossibility; proof in SM-B). Let venture value be generated by any admissible nurturing environment satisfying SH (respectively ED). Then no functions gg, hh and no merging rule Φ\Phi non-decreasing in its institutional argument (respectively its venture argument) represent the value ordering: f=Φ(g(P,R,S),h(A))f = \Phi\big(g(P,R,S),\, h(A)\big) fails on a pair of live, interior states. The argument is short enough to state. Under SH the value comparison of two institutional profiles reverses across two live venture states. A single score reads the institution through one index h(A)h(A): at the early state the representation forces h(A)>h(A′)h(A) > h(A'), and monotonicity of Φ\Phi then forces the composite to rank AA above A′A' at every venture state, including the late one, where the true ranking has reversed. No calibration escapes: nothing is assumed about gg and hh beyond existence, so the exclusion covers every weighting a committee might tune — multiplication, weighted addition, geometric means, CES mixes, min-gated and lexicographic hybrids, and every monotone recalibration. Under ED the same dichotomy runs through the venture index. A composite not monotone in its own indices defeats the purpose of scoring, so the demanded object does not exist. The committee of Section 1 is not miscalibrating an instrument; it is calibrating a nonexistent one.

Nor are the dynamics decoration: on a static domain f=min⁡(PRS,κ(A))f = \min(\mathrm{PRS}, \kappa(A)) survives annihilation and monotonicity; it is stage-contingency that kills it (SM-B.4). And the witnesses are interior, live states: pre-screened portfolios, not boundary pathologies.

Corollary 3.2 (portfolio impossibility; SM-B.5). If the portfolio spans technology domains rich enough that some institutional axis is operative for one seed’s environment and inert for another’s, then no environment-blind score F(P,R,S;A)F(P,R,S;A) — separable or not — represents value across the portfolio. The proof is immediate; the content is the domain condition: richness defeats the rule exactly as in preference aggregation (Arrow, 1963).

What survives is what committees should build instead. Additive shortlisting αPRS+βERS\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS} remains available — not as measurement (C1′ forbids that) but as a decision aid with public political weights (α,β)(\alpha, \beta) and a liveness guard against ranking corpses. Conditional gating — rank ventures by PRS\mathrm{PRS} only where institutions clear stated thresholds (A7≥LA_7 \geq L, say) — respects both ledgers without merging them. Governance separation routes project money by the venture ledger, capacity-building money by the institutional ledger. Impossibility, correctly read, is design guidance.

The result sits in a recognizable lineage: Arrow (1963) established the template — individually reasonable requirements, jointly unsatisfiable. New, to our knowledge, are the location and the mechanism: aggregation across observation levels, defeated by two observable facts about nurseries inside a registered channel structure. Evolutionary economics argued informally that selection environments and organizational routines are incommensurable (Nelson and Winter, 1982); Theorem 3 is that intuition made exact, with named premises and testable consequences (SM-B.9).

6. Retrospective calibration: eight projects

We now put numbers — deliberately coarse ones — behind the framework. The data are one deep-tech venture studio’s operating records with Japanese universities and national laboratories, 2007–2026: eight projects — five with decisions taken and twenty-four-month outcomes complete, one recently founded (right-censored), and two approaching scheduled decision points (readings reported, outcomes pending). Five disclosures govern what follows. The cases are composites: details altered, institutions anonymized as types, fidelity maintained on the variables the model reads. The exercise is retrospective calibration, not validation: outcomes were known when the rubrics were scored, so agreement demonstrates organization of known history, not prediction — the registered, prospective test is Section 7’s business. The sample is selected: one studio’s pipeline, with survivorship and idiosyncrasy we do not pretend to correct at N=8N = 8; the section’s role is illustration under honest labels. The concordance accounting is asymmetric by construction: of the six decisions so far taken, four were taken by parties other than the studio — T, Y, and K, which the instrument reads, and Q, which it cannot — while the studio-involved cases (R, L, and the pending M and N) are nearly definitionally concordant — the model formalizes the studio’s own decision process — so the effective externally-decided retrodiction sample is four cases, three readable, and the section should be cited at that size. And the timeline carries a circularity we can bound but not remove: the decisions at T, Y, K, and Q — and the studio’s early involvement at R — predate any version of the model (formalized 2025–2026), while the most recent readings (L, M, N) were taken in an environment already informed by its precursors; concordance there is evidence about the model-plus-studio bundle, not an independent test.

An accounting is owed of what the panel does not contain. No discordant retrospective reading was omitted: the projects reported are the projects scored; no case was dropped for disagreeing with its outcome. The twenty-four-month window is an early-trajectory classifier, not a terminal outcome: by then a pre-founding decision has revealed the shape of the trajectory it produced, and that shape is all the class claims to read. Its known failure mode is the slow domain: in drug discovery or advanced materials, “stagnant at twenty-four months” can be the normal trajectory of a good project. The registry therefore carries domain-specific outcome-class definitions, and longer-horizon re-reads at forty-eight and sixty months audit the early classifier against the horizons its slowest domains run on; SM-D.5 states the definitions and the mitigation.

The limitations are short because the design already stated most of them: N=8N = 8, one studio, one national ecosystem, retrospective scoring, composite disclosure. Two defects no amount of care removes from a hindsight design deserve their own clause: the panel is single-rater and outcome-aware — one scorer, who knew every completed outcome. The registry escapes them prospectively: two independent raters, blind to outcomes, reliability reported (SM-B.6.5(2)), and outcome classes adjudicated under the deposited definitions by a party other than the forecaster. What the section establishes is deliberately bounded: the two-ledger system can be scored from records that exist before day zero, its readings organize known outcomes without epicycles, and the composite alternatives fail on the same records in exactly the modes the theory names. What it does not establish is prediction; for that the model must put its thresholds on record before outcomes arrive — the program to which we now turn.

7. Policy implications and a research program

For evaluators the instruction is negative and liberating: retire the institution-adjusted single ranking — Theorem 3 shows the object it approximates does not exist. What replaces it is explicit structure. Where a one-dimensional shortlist is required, use αPRS+βERS\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS} as a decision aid, liveness precondition in force, (α,β)(\alpha, \beta) published as the political trade-off they are. Where structure permits, prefer conditional gating: rank ventures by the venture ledger within the population whose institutions clear stated thresholds. Both devices carry their assumptions on the outside.

Neither device is untried. Horizon Europe’s standard evaluation — three award criteria, each scored 0–5 with thresholds of 3 per criterion and 10 overall [verify at S6] — is a gated-additive design, Table 1’s fourth family fielded at the scale of the world’s largest research program. The United Kingdom’s Knowledge Exchange Framework publishes perspective-level dashboards and deliberately declines to compress them into a composite ranking [verify at S6]: a national funding body operating, in production, the refusal Theorem 3 licenses. HEInnovate, the European Commission–OECD self-assessment for entrepreneurial universities, scores dimensions overlapping SM-D’s axes [verify at S6] but is a self-diagnostic: no aggregation theory licenses a total, no audited evidence standard disciplines the scoring, nothing links the profile to a venture-level object. The AUTM and ASTP surveys observe activity metrics — disclosures, licenses, spin-outs — downstream of the capabilities ERS\mathrm{ERS} scores [verify at S6]: complements to a capability rubric, not substitutes. The correspondence table in SM-D.6 states the position instrument by instrument. What axiomatization adds is the aggregation license (Theorem 1), the gate (Proposition 1), the audited missing-data discipline (E3), and the causal linkage to a venture ledger (the C3′ carriers); what it borrows from KEF and Horizon Europe is the existence proof. Our devices sharpen what the best committees already grope toward — and name what those designs still get wrong: merging layers, imputing unknowns silently, leaving gates unanchored, and yielding, each budget cycle, to the ministry’s demand for the composite.

For research institutions, the additive form is the point: ERS\mathrm{ERS} is a gap-diagnosis instrument, not a beauty contest. Its decomposability tells a technology-transfer office which axis, improved next, moves readiness most — and Proposition 1 names the binding constraint: capabilities behind a missing legal pathway are idle capacity. The gate is not a rules-first sequencing instruction: in observed practice the first deals precede and produce the rules, and bylaws drafted in advance of any deal are a known pathology, blocking the deals they were written to enable; what the gate demands is an exercised pathway (SM-D’s level-2 anchor), scoring the residue of deal–rule co-evolution rather than its order. The temptation the ledger removes is the tournament reading: a published national ERS\mathrm{ERS} ranking would recreate at the institutional layer the pathology Theorem 3 diagnoses at the merged layer.

One deployed instrument deserves a paragraph of its own, because the model prices it exactly and the price is negative. Programs that condition their grants on incorporation within the program period are negative timing instruments: they impose an artificial deadline on the founding option, and a deadline forces exercise at the boundary — the rule collapses toward “found iff the flow is positive” (SM-C’s Marshallian deadline exercise) at whatever macro state prevails: the instrument manufactures founding at σSU<θσ*(k;F)\sigma_{SU} < \theta_\sigma^*(k;F) by construction. This is how zombies are made at scale: the instrument pays for the premature GO; the tailwind keeps the venture alive without milestone progress — Section 6’s T-type — and the program’s success metric, incorporations per period, reads the damage as achievement. The repair changes the deadline’s object, not the program’s generosity: deadline the milestones, not the incorporation — tranche funds on maturation evidence and let the founding date float — or run the deadline on the earmarked pre-entry money, where expiry prices the wait correctly. A program that counts incorporations buys incorporations; a program that wants ventures should count maturation.

The composite’s persistence has a reason, and it is not statistical naivety: the composite is an accountability artifact. A single published ranking hands the program officer an audit trail, while two ledgers plus deliberation reads, inside an agency, as more discretion — what agencies fear. The answer lives in the paper’s own apparatus: gates are more auditable than continuous coefficients. “We fund only ventures whose institutions clear stated, published thresholds” survives an audit better than any tuned weighting, because the question “why this cutoff?” has a stated answer — the anchor sheet and the precondition argument behind Proposition 1 — whereas “why this weight?” provably has none. Public weights, liveness guards, and institutional gates carry their assumptions on the outside; that, not the absence of judgment, is what audit-defensibility means.

For policy design, Proposition 2 converts slogans into channels that do not all point the same way — the point. Instruments that read as synonyms act on different arguments of the founding threshold: subsidies and demand commitments raise PP; shared facilities lower BB; standardized, low-cost founding procedures lower II — a fifth lever, cheap because administrative. Program credibility has a precise address: durability of the aligned regime lowers the bar in the aligned regime and — the case that matters to a founder still warming — in the regime she may switch out of; a stickier dormant regime raises its bar. Credibility of the good state is a founding subsidy without a yen of transfer; stickiness in general is not — though persistence is a belief object, not a dial, exercised through commitment devices whose fiscal cost is the true price of the “free” subsidy. Funding instruments require the finest distinction: the model prices two monies. Gap funds, bridging fellowships, laboratory residence are earmarked pre-entry money: they extend the option’s life, funding a viable project’s WAIT\mathrm{WAIT} — the Boyle–Guthrie channel, buying patience. Committed post-entry finance — follow-on commitments, matching funds, a patient first customer — lowers the founding bar itself, and dominates where runway binds: Figure 3’s threshold curve is dip-shaped in runway, so the same yen is a founding subsidy on one side of the dip and a patience subsidy on the other. A program that wants earlier founding should commit post-entry money; one that wants better-timed founding should fund the wait. One honesty completes the catalogue: real instruments load on several channels — a gap fund is slack and purchased maturation in one envelope, tranche design choosing the mix — so the taxonomy’s value is that loading can be chosen deliberately rather than inherited by accident.

When the ministry nonetheless demands the composite, the deliverable fits in three items a program officer can lift as written. First, a venture shortlist ranked by PRS\mathrm{PRS} within the population whose institutions clear the published gates — A7≥LA_7 \geq L, plus any program-specific axes. Second, where a single ordering is compulsory, the additive decision aid, liveness guard in force, weights published as the political trade-off they are. Third, the two-ledger budget split: project funding by the venture ledger, capacity funding by the institutional ledger, no line item requiring the number Theorem 3 forbids. The audit file answers “why these cutoffs?” with anchor sheets and “why no weights?” with a theorem — a better position than any composite has ever defended.

The research program — Before Zero Studies — is registered rather than promised. Its falsifiers are stated: the Corollary 3.1 diagnostics at a single family-wise error rate, with pre-registered power and equivalence bounds (SM-B.6); channel completeness (C3′) via GMM overidentification on institutional shocks; and a prospective registry in which GO/WAIT/NO_GO\mathrm{GO}/\mathrm{WAIT}/\mathrm{NO\_GO} readings are filed before outcomes arrive, scored on twenty-four-month outcome classes, with a pre-committed Brier-score floor beneath which the model is declared to have failed (Popper, 1959; Lakatos, 1970). Protocols, rubrics, and outcome definitions are deposited on the Open Science Framework; the registry opens with Section 6’s studio pipeline and is built to admit other ecosystems.

The declared boundary bears repeating: deep-tech ventures, Japanese university and national-laboratory origins, the pre-founding stage. Portability — other national systems, software-dominant ventures, corporate spin-outs — is the registry’s question, not this paper’s claim. Within its boundary the framework is complete: measurable with instruments that exist, impossible to collapse into the single score practice keeps demanding, operable by a rule whose every parameter has an economic name.

8. Conclusion

Deep-tech ventures are largely decided before they legally exist, and the deciding stage has lacked a measurement theory. This paper built one: a venture ledger that multiplies because ventures die of their weakest link; an institutional ledger that adds — uniquely, by Theorem 1 — because institutions are portfolios of substitutable capabilities; a macro state that links them; and a proof (Theorem 3) that the two ledgers cannot be merged into any monotone single score once institutional capabilities act stage-contingently through the venture’s dynamics. The go/wait decision emerged as an optimal-stopping rule with threshold endogenous to project economics (Theorem 4), and eight retrospectively calibrated projects illustrated the system’s readings and the live failure modes of the composites it replaces.

Before day zero, value is real but unmeasured. The Before Zero Model makes it measurable — and the impossibility theorem stands guard over the practice, ensuring the two ledgers are not quietly re-merged into the convenient number that cannot exist. If the registered program falsifies the framework, the field will have learned something definite; if it survives, evaluation practice acquires foundations where it currently has habits. Either way, Before Zero Studies begins on an empirical footing rather than a rhetorical one.

Practice reading of the axioms. Each axiom is a statement about technology-transfer practice before it is mathematics. E1: committees do assess bylaws without auditing the incubator — the contribution of one axis to institutional readiness is judged without knowing the level of every other. The practice content of E3 — honest gap diagnosis requires that ignorance stay visible without being rewarded — and of E5 — an assessor’s calibrated trade-off between two capabilities does not depend on which further axes happened to be observable — is already carried in their statements above and in the observation protocol of SM-A.5.

SM-A.5 Remark: audited observation and strategic non-disclosure

This subsection records the observation protocol E3 presupposes; without it, the design would invite the behavior it is meant to prevent.

Under self-reported missingness the KobsK_{\mathrm{obs}} renormalization is strategically gameable. Withholding axis mm moves the score from 100∑KwkAk/4∑Kwk100\sum_{K}w_kA_k/4\sum_{K}w_k to the same expression over K\{m}K\setminus\{m\} — strictly larger exactly when AmA_m lies below the weighted average of the other observed axes, precisely when disclosure would hurt. The mechanism is the denominator: a disclosed weakness leaves numerator and denominator together — little above, full weight below — while ⊥\bot removes both at once. An institution whose weak axis would score low therefore raises its ERS by declining to evidence that axis. The moment ERS carries funding or reputational stakes, non-disclosure becomes the dominant move, and E3 — designed as an honesty device — becomes an opacity subsidy.

The protocol therefore fixes three rules. (i) ⊥\bot is assigned under the assessor’s evidence standard, not the institution’s: an axis is ⊥\bot only when evidence is genuinely unavailable after audit. (ii) Declined-to-evidence is not ⊥\bot: it is coded at the rubric floor (level 00) and the axis remains in KobsK_{\mathrm{obs}} — worst-case coding, the standard response of credit-rating practice to withheld information. Under (i)–(ii) non-disclosure can only lower the score, and the incentive reverses to disclosure. (iii) The report is the pair (ERS,Kobs)(\mathrm{ERS},K_{\mathrm{obs}}), and stripping KobsK_{\mathrm{obs}} is out of protocol: scores computed on different observed sets are not on a common scale. A shortlist that compares institutions across observed sets must either stratify by KobsK_{\mathrm{obs}} or use the decision-conservative completion — unobserved axes at the floor — disclosed as such.

The connection to Rubin (1976) is exact. Renormalization over KobsK_{\mathrm{obs}} is representationally harmless only when the missingness mechanism is ignorable — when which axes are missing carries no information about their levels. Audited ⊥\bot approximates ignorability by construction: after (i)–(ii), missingness reflects the assessor’s access, not the institution’s choice. Self-reported ⊥\bot does not — it is missingness selected on the missing value itself, the canonical non-ignorable mechanism — and a score renormalized over it inherits the selection.

Remarks. (i) Scope. Nothing was assumed about gg and hh — no monotonicity, no continuity; the only structure used is that the institutional information reaches the score through a single index used monotonically (under ED, symmetrically, the venture information). The excluded class therefore contains every composite in evaluation practice — pure multiplication, weighted addition, geometric and CES blends, min/max gates, lexicographic hybrids — and every monotone recalibration of any of them. Practice endorses monotonicity in both arguments — a better venture score, or a better institution score, should never lower the composite — so either richness condition alone is fatal.

  1. Why a static axiomatization cannot deliver this. On the static domain, weakly monotone separable scores consistent with annihilation and institutional monotonicity exist: f=min⁡(PRS,κ(A))f=\min\big(PRS,\kappa(A)\big), with κ\kappa any strictly increasing index calibrated to the institution’s induced response, vanishes with the venture ledger and never decreases in any argument, and an axiom system that constrains ff pointwise cannot exclude it. What excludes it is stage-contingency of the dynamics: under SH the value ordering of two institutions genuinely reverses across venture strata, and no single institutional index — however cleverly calibrated — can lie on both sides of a reversal. The dynamic foundation is therefore not a technical reformulation of Section 4 but the source of the theorem’s content.

  2. Tightness. Where the channel is inoperative (λ\lambda free of AA), fef_e does not depend on AA and Φ(g,const)\Phi(g,\mathrm{const}) with g=feg=f_e trivially represents ≿e\succsim_e. The premises are observable: SH and ED belong to the registered program of Section 7, so the theorem’s empirical discipline is explicit.

  3. Boundary versus interior. Lemma SM-B.2’s obstruction lives on the death boundary; Theorem 3’s lives at live states — both witnesses have min⁡(P,S)>0\min(P,S)>0. The impossibility bites exactly where committees operate: on pre-screened live portfolios from which the corpses have already been removed.

SM-B.6.5 Estimation caveats (inherited verbatim by the registry protocol)

(1) Ordered outcome and the cross-stratum comparison problem. The registry outcome is a three-class ordered variable, while Propositions SM-B.1–B.2 are derived on a linear (latent-index) link. Ordered logit/probit coefficients are identified only up to stratum-specific residual scale, so equality of index coefficients across strata is not testable from ordered-model coefficients alone: apparent slope instability can be manufactured, and true instability masked, by residual-variance differences across strata (Mood, 2010) [needs S6 verification]. Prescription: primary estimation by linear probability models on the outcome-class indicators — the estimands of the propositions in the level metric, which is also where the annihilated-cell prediction (b) lives — or by the latent-y*y^{*} formulation with pre-registered equivalence bounds on cross-stratum scale ratios; ordered-model coefficients are reported as secondary descriptives only.

(2) Generated regressors and errors-in-variables. The stratifier and the institutional regressor are estimated indices — rubric-scored, calibration-mapped. Classical measurement error in vv misclassifies strata and attenuates within-stratum slopes toward zero, an artifact that mimics the annihilated-cell null and blurs the cross-stratum profile; error in aa attenuates every institutional slope. The protocol therefore requires an errors-in-variables strategy, not a disclaimer: a repeat-scoring reliability subsample (two independent raters, blind to outcomes, with reliability reported), regression-calibration or instrumenting one rater’s score with the other’s, and full two-step bootstrap of the resulting standard errors. The annihilated-cell null is evidentially meaningful only in the reliability-corrected fit and only alongside strictly positive corrected slopes at operative strata: the registered pattern is the contrast, never the null alone.

(3) Harmonized error rates. The diagnostic family consists of exactly two registered contrasts — (i)’s pooled-versus-partial sign contrast (Proposition SM-B.1) and the slope-equality Wald test (SM-B.6.4; signatures (ii) and (iii) are one test stated twice) — held at a single family-wise error rate α=0.05\alpha=0.05 by Holm’s step-down. SM-B.8 Certification effects and the channel restriction (expanded)

Section 4 states in three sentences why certification effects do not break C3′; this subsection preserves the full argument as drafted for the main text, unchanged.

The channel restriction must face, rather than wave at, the strongest empirical objection to it. A substantial literature documents certification effects: affiliation with prominent partners raises young ventures’ survival and speeds their capital acquisition (Stuart, Hoang and Hybels, 1999); entrepreneurs accept discounted terms to obtain high-reputation venture backing (Hsu, 2004); early-stage public grants causally improve subsequent private financing (Howell, 2017) — and every practitioner in our context knows the folk version, the premium a national-university seal commands. Do these findings break C3′? The class answers with a distinction between price and value. What certification demonstrably moves is the venture’s access to resources: capital arrives sooner, on better terms, from better partners. In the model those are trajectory consequences — endorsement and access operate on the slack FF, the survival hazard, and the exposure to σSU\sigma_{SU} innovations, carriers inside the licensed channel, and SM-D routes exactly the axes that carry certification in practice (A4A_4, A5A_5, A8A_8) through them. What the class excludes is only the residual: a premium on measured value at a fixed venture trajectory — a halo that changes what a committee writes down without changing what the venture does or survives. That residual is precisely what the Section 7 overidentification test estimates; certification effects that work through trajectories sit inside its null, not against it. And the theorem degrades gracefully if the test rejects: Theorem 3’s proof uses only the reversal condition SH, a directly observable property of value orderings, so a documented direct channel would change the class’s interpretation — value partly constituted by perception — without rescuing the single score, unless the direct premium were strong and uniform enough to undo every stage-contingent reversal: itself an empirical question, and one the registered program is built to price.

SM-B.9 Lineage and location of the result

Section 4 compresses the result’s genealogy to the Arrow template and the Nelson–Winter antecedent; this subsection preserves the fuller statement of where the theorem does and does not innovate. Arrow (1963) showed that individually reasonable axioms on preference aggregation across people are jointly unsatisfiable; Holmström and Milgrom (1991) showed that single-index incentives distort effort across tasks; the index-number literature has long known that no single deflator serves all purposes. The boundary lemma’s mathematics — annihilation defeats strict separability — is noncompensatory-aggregation territory (Fishburn, 1976; Bouyssou and Marchant, 2007), and the failure of the institutional sector to separate from the venture sector can be phrased in the classical separability vocabulary (Gorman, 1968; Blackorby, Primont and Russell, 1978); SM-B.2 develops both connections, which are therefore only named here. What is new, to our knowledge, is the location and the mechanism of the impossibility: aggregation across observation levels — the project and the institution that hosts it — defeated not by a normative axiom but by two observable facts about how nurseries act on seeds, inside a value model whose channel structure is itself registered for test (SM-B.7). The nearest antecedents in evolutionary economics (Nelson and Winter, 1982) argued informally that selection environments and organizational routines are incommensurable; Theorem 3 is that intuition made exact, with named premises and testable consequences.

The old heuristic’s “forced entry” branch lives in this world, now derived rather than asserted.

The two conventions bracket institutional reality (external founding finance versus gap-fund-paid founding transactions); C.3(iii) derives the funds comparative static under each, and they differ qualitatively — under (E) the threshold curve is dip-shaped in FF, under (J) it is increasing throughout (C.5). Earmarked, non-fungible pre-entry funds (interpretation). This is the institutional reality of the pre-founding stage the paper models, and we state it as the maintained reading rather than leave it implicit. Waiting is therefore not costless: it generates no objective flow, but it burns the earmarked stock at rate B−RnetB-R_{\mathrm{net}} and thereby shortens the option’s life — the deadline channel of C.3(iii).

C.4 The TRL gate: exogeneity stated, defended, and delimited

Assumption (TG) (exogenous deterministic maturation). gTRLg_{TRL} is a deterministic, exogenous, non-decreasing {0,1}\{0,1\}-valued function of time: the maturation ramp neither responds to (σ,k,F)(\sigma,k,F), nor is chosen, nor fails. The earlier draft stated conditional independence σSU⟂gTRL∣k\sigma_{SU}\perp g_{TRL}\mid k; as the referee observes, for a deterministic gate that statement is vacuous — a deterministic function is independent of everything — so we now state the assumption that does the work. (TG) is an assumption about the world, and the main text’s “the factorization is a theorem rather than an assumption” is softened accordingly (MAIN_EDITS.md): the factorization is a theorem given (TG).

Defense. Pre-founding technology maturation in the university and national-laboratory setting runs on administrative clocks: stage-gated grant programs, facility construction schedules, doctoral and postdoctoral timelines — instruments committed at award and rarely re-optimized at the frequency at which macro regimes move. Development intensity is likewise committed, not continuously chosen: before day zero there is no firm to reallocate resources inside. And the certified gate is ratchet-like by construction of TRL practice: certification does not regress; technical setbacks appear in the venture ledger as slower RR and worse SS — which is where the model’s survival accounting (C.0.1) books technical mortality — rather than as gate reversals. Within this paper’s scope (TG) is therefore a reasonable first-order description, and it is auditable: the registered program logs scheduled against realized gate dates, and a systematic correlation of maturation-schedule deviations with regime states would reject (TG) directly.

Limitation. Each relaxation breaks the factorization, in an instructive direction. Regime-dependent maturation (hot fields accelerate through funding cycles) couples the gate to kk: the policy no longer factorizes and θ*\theta^* becomes gate-state-dependent. Endogenous development intensity turns the problem into joint control-and-stopping. A stochastic gate with failure risk introduces the waiting-to-learn motive — the compound/sequential R&D option of McGrath (1997) and staged-investment analyses — which is absent from this model by design: here waiting reveals macro information only, never technical information. The tagline “two non-compensable failure modes” is thus a consequence of (TG), not a discovery about deep tech, and the main text now presents it as such.

Each axis is scored on a five-level ordinal rubric (0–4) anchored by observable institutional states — documents, staffed functions, and operating track records — rather than judgment scales. The full anchor sheets are maintained in the study’s operating protocol; the table below summarizes what each axis observes and the logic of its anchors. Blank-sheet checklists are deliberately not reproduced: the rubric is designed to be scored from evidence, and the registered program (Section 7 of the main text) deposits the scoring protocol with the registry.

The axis-7 anchor ladder (levels 0–4). Because axis 7 carries the Proposition 1 gate — the difference between an institution scored and an institution gated to idle — its full anchor ladder is published here rather than summarized. The rubric observes four rule blocks (spin-out approval; conflict-of-interest management; equity policy; leave and return policy) in two institutional states: written (rules formally adopted) and exercised (at least one founding processed through the pathway). The anchors:

  • Level 0. No written rules and no exercised pathway: no spin-out has been approved by any route, formal or improvised. Only this state — not in the game by any door — gates to zero.
  • Level 1. An exercised improvised pathway without written rules — at least one spin-out approved ad hoc, at founder-borne friction, with nothing codified — or written drafts covering at most one of the four blocks that have never processed a founding. An improvisation that has produced a company scores 1, not 0: an exercised de-facto pathway is a capability, however costly its exercise.
  • Level 2 (the gate value L=2L=2 of Proposition 1). Core rules written and exercised: spin-out approval and conflict-of-interest rules formally adopted, and at least one founding processed under them. Equity and leave may remain case-by-case. The gate is anchored at written-and-exercised core, not at completeness: unexercised model bylaws, however complete on paper, do not clear it, because administrator-drafted rules that no deal has tested are a documented pathology rather than a pathway (Section 7 of the main text).
  • Level 3. Complete written rule set across all four blocks, exercised, with minor gaps of detail in equity or leave — equity terms negotiated case-by-case inside a written framework, say, or leave provisions adopted but not yet used.
  • Level 4. Complete, exercised bylaws across all four blocks (the table’s anchor): every block written, every block used at least once, terms stable across recent foundings.

Two boundaries carry the design. The 0/10/1 boundary splits a state the summary table previously conflated: “no rules and no de-facto pathway” and “no rules but exercised improvisation” are different institutions — the second founds companies, slowly — and only the first is outside the game. The 1/21/2 boundary is the gate, and it is an exercised-pathway test on the two blocks without which no founding closes (approval and COI), consistent with the sequencing discussion of Section 7: the first deals typically precede and produce the rules, and the gate scores the residue of that co-evolution, not the order in which it happened.

Correlated measurement. At mid-tier institutions, axes A2A_2, A4A_4, A5A_5, and A8A_8 are frequently staffed by one small office: the same few people run licensing, the corporate interface, the gap-fund window, and the government-program channel. The four scores are then not measurement-independent even though the four capabilities are conceptually distinct, and the implication is stated rather than hidden: a thin office caps several axes at once, so the portfolio reading of Theorem 1 survives — the axes remain distinct objects with distinct evidence anchors — but independence of the measurement errors does not, and a user treating the eight scores as eight independent observations is over-counting evidence. The protocol’s response is twofold. The evidence anchors are function-specific — a license negotiated, a joint-research contract converted, a gap-fund tranche disbursed, a program award administered — so one office can genuinely hold all four track records, and then deserves all four scores. And the reliability subsample of SM-B.6.5(2) estimates the cross-axis error correlation directly, so the registered diagnostics inherit a measured, not assumed, error structure.

A deliberate omission. Leadership commitment and faculty incentive design — the career consequences of founding: promotion weight, evaluation penalties, the institution’s live enthusiasm — is by wide practitioner testimony among the strongest determinants of whether seeds come forward at all. It is deliberately not an axis, because it resists document-anchored observation at the rubric discipline this supplement enforces: commitment statements are cheap to produce, incentive rules are gameable on paper, and the reliable residue of real commitment is hard to distinguish from the residue the existing axes already score. It therefore enters the ledger through what it produces — exercised pathways (A7A_7), EIR placements and matching track records (A6A_6), scouting throughput (A1A_1) — rather than through what it declares. The cost is acknowledged: an institution on the eve of a genuine leadership turn scores no better than one without it, until the turn produces evidence.

The eight projects of Section 6 are composites drawn from one studio’s operating records (2007–2026). SM-D.3 Momenta series construction (summary)

The three momenta are constructed from observable series per domain: μA\mu_A from publication dynamics in the seed’s research domain; μI\mu_I from industrial commitment indicators (corporate participation, procurement signals); μG\mu_G from policy budgets and program calls. Each series is smoothed, growth-transformed, and mapped to a bounded momentum scale before entering σSU=∏(μ+1)3−1\sigma_{SU} = \sqrt[3]{\prod (\mu + 1)} - 1. Series definitions and windows are part of the deposited protocol; regime boundaries (S0/S1/S2S_0/S_1/S_2) are calibrated, not estimated, in this paper (Tier discipline of Section 3). Domain boundaries — the definition of the seed’s domain, over which all three series are computed — are fixed ex ante in the deposited protocol and are contestable on the record: the domain is the most gameable object in the system, since whoever draws the boundary chooses the tailwind, and the protocol treats it as such (boundaries fixed before scoring, amendments logged, momenta recomputed under the registered boundary rather than the advocate’s).

SM-D.4 Venture-ledger scoring disciplines and the disclosure layer split

This subsection records the operational scoring conventions of the venture ledger and the disclosure architecture, both stated in compressed form in Section 3 of the main text; SM-D.1 is their institutional-ledger counterpart.

How (P,R,S)(P, R, S) are read. Each venture-ledger factor is scored on a five-level ordinal rubric anchored by observable states — evidence documents and demonstrated capabilities, not judgment scales — and every reading is taken at the decision date, from the records available at that date: for RR, the documented maturation state of technology, team, and commercialization preparations (a prototype demonstrated outside its originating laboratory, a key hire signed, a regulatory pre-consultation held); for SS, the evidenced mortality structure (single-point technical dependencies, team fragility, the documented state of the market window); for PP, the two diligence objects of the main text’s construct — addressable demand and attainable unit economics — whose anchors are kept separate so that a healthy reading on one cannot be quoted for the other.

Three scoring disciplines. Information sets are dated. Retrospective scoring (Section 6 of the main text) admits only evidence knowable at the decision date, and the registry’s prospective readings are timestamped before outcomes arrive (Section 7), so the information set is auditable in both directions. Measurement capacity is endogenous. Scoring PP at rubric discipline requires unit-economics diligence — capacity of exactly the kind axis A1A_1 measures — so the quality of a venture-ledger reading depends on who reads it: an argument for scoring at the agency or studio level rather than by self-assessment. Domain boundaries are fixed ex ante. The seed’s domain — the object over which the macro momenta are computed — is set in the deposited protocol before scoring and is contestable on the record; the rule, and the reason the domain is the most gameable object in the system, are recorded in SM-D.3 and are not repeated here.

The disclosure layer split. The disclosure architecture is a declared layer split, and the split is stated as a split. On the public side sit the theorems, the rubrics, the anchor logic, and the coding conventions: all axis scores are ordinal rubrics (Stevens, 1946) anchored by observable states, not judgment scales; the distinction between “unknown” and “not started” is coded explicitly and propagates to KobsK_{obs} (Little and Rubin, 2019), under the audited observation protocol of SM-A.5; and institutions are reported as anonymized types and projects as composites (Section 6 of the main text). On the proprietary side sit the studio’s fitted calibration constants — the mapping of rubric levels into the model’s numerical ranges — treated as a calibration procedure and reported as method, not as results, both to respect proprietary operating data and because the theorems of the paper are invariant to them; the registry’s deposit-under-embargo mechanics for these constants are given in SM-D.2. Everything an adopting evaluator needs in order to rescore from its own records is on the public side of the split.

SM-D.5 Outcome classes and the twenty-four-month window

The twenty-four-month outcome classification of Table 2 (progressing / stagnant / terminated; right-censored where applicable) is an early-trajectory classifier, not a terminal outcome measure. Progressing requires documented milestone or revenue advance against the decision-date plan; stagnant is survival without such advance, whatever pays for the survival; terminated is wind-up. The classifier reads what funding purchased, not where funding came from: a competitive, milestone-gated public grant that buys maturation milestones reads progressing (Project R), while tailwind-funded persistence with no milestone movement is the class definition of stagnation (Project T) — the distinction that keeps the T reading (zombie) and the R reading (progressing) from being an asymmetry of sympathy rather than of evidence.

The window’s known failure mode is the slow domain. In drug discovery or advanced materials, “stagnant at twenty-four months” can be the normal early trajectory of a good project, so a domain-uniform window would systematically misclassify slow-good ventures in exactly the domains this paper claims for its scope. The registry therefore carries domain-specific outcome-class definitions — drug discovery flagged provisional, as Table 2’s note records for its readiness mapping — and the misclassification risk of slow-good ventures is registered as a known limitation with a stated mitigation: longer-horizon re-reads at forty-eight and sixty months as the registry matures, so that the early classifier is itself audited against the horizons its slowest domains run on. Re-read outcomes at the longer horizons are adjudicated under the same deposited definitions, by a party other than the forecaster, as the main text’s registry commitments state.
