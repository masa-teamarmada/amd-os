## Executive verdict

The P0 enrichment is a real improvement, but it still does not clear the bar
that matters: a reader does not yet feel compelled to keep turning pages.

The new material is directionally right. Ch01 now understands that a payment
refusal can be more useful than a polite "interesting." Ch06 now separates a
paid signal that may increase survival probability from a short-term paid
detour that may damage the future. Ch09 now lets the investor ask the right
question about refusal and budget path. Ch19 now puts non-payment, two paid
options, and RESOURCE_SHIFT in the same survival conversation. Ch04/13/18 now
test external CEO fit through repeat-back and bad-news handling instead of
resume worship. Ch21 clearly marks the narrative close and Ch22-Ch24 finally
carry Field Toolkit labels.

But the manuscript still behaves like a responsible explainer that has learned
to open sections with scenes. It is not yet a trade book that makes the reader
worry about a person, a document, a relationship, or a decision across chapter
boundaries.

The core failure remains: consequence is introduced, then neutralized too
quickly by correct explanation.

Cold verdict:
- P0 enrichment improved the evidence discipline.
- P0 enrichment did not yet create enough dread, cost, or emotional carryover.
- The paid/refusal scenes are useful as concept proof, but not yet dramatic
engines.
- Ch04/08/13/18 now repeat the same founder-function lesson in several rooms.
- Ch22-Ch24 are better separated, but the ending still risks cooling the reader
because the toolkit reopens story heat without a designed reference-mode shift.
- Next step is not line polish. It is a targeted narrative consequence rewrite
of Ch01/04/06/08/09/13/18/19/21-24 plus a theory-residue compression pass on
Ch12/15/16/17/18/20.

Current appeal score after P0 enrichment: 6.7 / 10.

This is better than the previous draft. It is still not "I need to read one
more chapter."

## What improved, but not enough

### Paid evidence is finally not treated as small-money doctrine

Ch01, Ch06, Ch09, and Ch19 now protect the author directive: small payment is
evidence only when it reveals pain, budget route, repeatability, and opportunity
cost. The draft no longer says, crudely, "earn early." It says payment, refusal,
and paid detour must be read.

That is the right idea.

But the scenes still feel too professionally useful. The reader sees the
distinction; the reader does not yet feel the room pay for misreading it.

What is missing:
- the moment when the inflated "customer" line has already traveled and must be
  walked back in front of someone;
- the researcher reacting not just with fatigue, but with changed trust;
- a support manager defending the stronger line because their program needs
  outcomes;
- a paid-service temptation that someone genuinely wants to take, not a quickly
  dismissed bad option;
- one lingering bruise after the payment refusal, rather than clean learning.

### External CEO fit is more concrete, but now overused

The repeat-back test is strong. The bad-news behavior test is strong. The phrase
"weak facts must stay weak" is one of the manuscript's best operating instincts.

But Ch04, Ch13, and Ch18 all now perform a similar lesson:
- external CEO is not a title;
- researcher's protected line must be repeated without strengthening;
- bad news must not be smoothed away;
- first ninety days should be function allocation, not role romance.

That is correct and repetitive.

The reader should not have to learn the same fit test three times. Ch04 should
make the reader feel the displacement fear. Ch13 should stage the actual
candidate misfit or partial repair. Ch18 should compress theory language and
become the definitive behavior test, or Ch13 and Ch18 should be merged in the
next architecture pass.

### Field Toolkit separation is a needed structural move

Ch21 now says the narrative closes and Ch22-Ch24 are Field Toolkit. That helps.
It prevents the main body from pretending the checklist is the emotional
ending.

But the transition is not yet elegant. Ch22 immediately starts with the young
commercialization lead at night and continues as prose. That pulls the reader
back into story mode after the story supposedly ended. The toolkit should feel
like a deliberately designed reference section, not a continuation that has
changed its title.

The end should do this:
1. Ch21 closes emotionally on the next researcher having a better first room.
2. A short Field Toolkit landing page tells the reader they are now in
   reference mode.
3. Toolkit A/B/C use terse, usable artifacts with compact filled examples.
4. The final question from Ch24 returns as a short coda, not as a manual
   paragraph.

## Where the new scenes still fail to pull the reader

### Ch01: payment refusal arrives, but does not damage the room

The refusal is useful. It reveals the budget owner and next condition. But the
chapter makes the refusal emotionally safe too quickly. The email refuses
payment, gives good reasons, and becomes helpful. The young lead updates the
material. The researcher is protected.

That is a nice learning loop, not a page-turner.

Rewrite need:
- Make the first inflated "customer candidate" line travel into another room
  before the refusal arrives.
- Put the refusal in tension with a support application, a pitch page, or a
  researcher who had started to believe the stronger line.
- Let one person resist including the refusal because it weakens the deck.
- Make the final update a trust-repair scene, not only an evidence discipline
  paragraph.

### Ch06: the two paid options are conceptually right, but the temptation is weak

The useful paid option and distracting paid option are exactly the right
structure. But the distracting option is too obviously a detour. The reader does
not feel the economic pressure to take it.

Rewrite need:
- Make the short-term paid service solve a real near-term problem: visible
  revenue for a support interview, proof of earning body, relief for a team
  worried about being seen as "just research."
- Let someone argue for it well.
- Make refusing it cost something: a weaker application line, a disappointed
  support manager, a researcher losing easy validation, or one missed meeting.
- Then make the accepted/kept paid path more operational: who pays, for what
  evaluation, through which budget, with what technical time cost.

### Ch09: investor scene sharpens, but still redeems too quickly

The investor asking "why did they not pay?" is the right scene. It should sting.
It currently stings for a page and then becomes a useful diagnosis.

Rewrite need:
- Let the investor first misunderstand or press too hard, so each actor hears a
  different wound.
- Make the young lead almost hide the refusal, not merely consider it.
- Name the exact sentence the team regrets bringing.
- Do not allow "what to do next" to arrive before the emotional misread is
  visible.
- Carry one damaged relationship into Ch10.

### Ch19: strongest P0 addition, still under-specific

Ch19 is the best of the enrichment chapters. Non-payment, two paid options,
J-curve caution, survival probability, and RESOURCE_SHIFT belong here.

But it still reads like a well-run workshop. The chapter needs more physical
specifics:
- what is the paid evaluation's deliverable;
- whose budget line would pay it;
- which researcher's day is consumed by the short-term service;
- what exact investor/showcase activity is cut;
- who is disappointed by the subtraction;
- which "not this month" line hurts.

Without those, RESOURCE_SHIFT is intelligent but not gripping.
