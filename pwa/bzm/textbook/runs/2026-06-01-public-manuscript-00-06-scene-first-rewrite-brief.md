## 1. Editorial Diagnosis

The current opening manuscript is externally readable and has already cleared
the first publication gate: it avoids company-promotion voice, internal tooling,
source-path language, and private event naming.

The remaining problem is narrative force.

The chapters are mostly correct essays. They explain the right lenses:
research-readiness separation, actor clocks, support isolation, disclosure
sequence, CEO-function decomposition, and incorporation timing. But the reader
does not yet feel one continuous field situation tightening from chapter to
chapter.

The rewrite should make the first six chapters behave like one continuous
reading experience:

1. A promising research result enters a support ecosystem.
2. The support ecosystem accelerates the project in visible ways.
3. Different actors read the same progress with different clocks.
4. The researcher receives mutually rational but emotionally contradictory role
   demands.
5. The GAP/VC/CEO scene reveals the missing object: unassigned CEO functions.
6. Once the project is pushed outward, disclosure and incorporation timing
   become consequence chapters, not isolated advice chapters.

The central editorial move is to promote current `05-gap-vc-ceo-function.md`
into the new Chapter 4. Current `04-before-disclosure.md` should move after it
and become the chapter that asks: once the researcher and research result have
been pushed outward, what must be protected before external conversations run
ahead of trust, IP, and role design?

### Chapter 1: Research Results Are Not Companies

#### Opening Scene

Start from the same composite project entering a support pipeline:

- The paper is promising.
- A patent discussion starts.
- One or two companies say the result is interesting.
- A support program accepts or encourages the project.
- A pitch deck begins to form.

The scene should create "false acceleration": everything looks like startup
progress, but company readiness has not been proven.

#### Central Tension

The field confuses evidence of research value with evidence of company
readiness. The researcher and support team feel momentum, yet the actual
business architecture remains thin.

#### Diagnosis

Separate:

- research strength;
- customer pain and buyer action;
- IP/disclosure safety;
- founder/CEO function;
- institutional support;
- capital readiness.

The diagnosis should not blame enthusiasm. It should show that enthusiasm is
real but insufficient.

#### Tool / Reusable Question

Use a two-column readiness split:

- What proves research excellence?
- What proves company readiness?

Reusable question:

> Which part of our confidence comes from the research, and which part comes
> from a company condition that has actually been tested?

be agreeing for different reasons and on different clocks. This opens Chapter

### Chapter 2: Different Clocks

#### Opening Scene

Use one milestone from the same composite case, for example:

- A support program wants a public-facing presentation by a certain date.
- The researcher wants to finish a paper or confirm reproducibility.
- The university needs invention/disclosure review.
- A company wants enough detail to judge PoC relevance.
- A VC wants to know who will run the company and what the next financing
  milestone proves.

The opening should show these clocks colliding in one week or one preparation
cycle, not as parallel textbook categories.

#### Central Tension

The same progress signal means different things to different actors:

- To the program, the pitch is progress.
- To the researcher, premature claims are risk.
- To the university, disclosure order matters.
- To the company, practical adoption conditions matter.
- To the VC, investable execution matters.

The tension is not "some actors are slow." It is that different rational clocks
are being forced into one startup timeline.

#### Diagnosis

Clock mismatch turns into distorted judgment when one clock dominates the
others. If the support-program clock dominates, the project may perform
startup-readiness. If the university clock dominates, it may never learn from
customers. If the investor clock dominates, the researcher may experience
readiness questions as personal rejection.

#### Tool / Reusable Question

Use a clock map:

- actor;
- what they are optimizing;
- what they must protect;
- what they need before moving;
- what harm occurs if their clock dominates.

Reusable question:

> Which clock is currently setting the pace, and which necessary clock is being
> treated as resistance?

#### Bridge To Chapter 3

End by saying clock mismatch becomes loneliness when no one integrates the
clocks into a shared decision map. This opens Chapter 3.

#### Use From Current Text

Keep:

- Researcher, university, company, VC/CVC, and public-program clock sections.
- The rule that "温度差" is too shallow; "時計が違う" is the better frame.
- The non-blaming tone.

Move / Compress:

  clock mismatch.

### Chapter 3: Support Can Isolate Researchers

#### Opening Scene

Show the researcher moving through several rooms:

- In a university or IP conversation, they are told to be cautious.
- In a company interview, they are asked for enough detail to judge usefulness.
- In an investor conversation preview, someone asks whether a real CEO exists.

Do not fully explode the GAP/VC/CEO contradiction yet. Build toward it.

#### Central Tension

The researcher is surrounded by help but not by integrated responsibility.
Support increases; role clarity does not. The emotional peak should be:

> I am being helped by many people, but I still do not know what I alone am
> supposed to carry.

#### Diagnosis

Support can isolate when each support actor optimizes a local output:

- polished pitch;
- safe IP process;
- more customer interviews;
- investor readiness;
- grant reporting;
- university outcome.

The missing object is a shared map of responsibilities and functions.

#### Tool / Reusable Question

Use a responsibility map:

- what the researcher must carry;
- what the institution must protect;
- what a support team can temporarily carry;
- what an external operator could carry;
- what no one currently carries.

Reusable question:

> Which unassigned function is currently appearing as the researcher's
> loneliness?

### Chapter 4: GAP/VC/CEO Central Hinge

#### Opening Scene

This is the chapter where the case zero fully appears.

Required beats:

1. A GAP fund or similar early commercialization program asks the researcher to
   present like a startup founder.
2. The pitch becomes clearer: customer, market, social impact, growth story.
3. The room rewards the researcher's entrepreneurial performance.
4. A later VC or financing conversation asks who will actually run the company.
5. An external executive suggestion appears.
6. The researcher hears the two demands as contradiction: "Am I the founder, or
   am I supposed to step aside?"
7. The chapter reveals that both rooms were asking incomplete questions.

The scene should include actor rationality:

- The GAP fund is trying to move research toward society.
- The VC is trying to judge execution risk.
- The university/support team is trying to create opportunity.
- The researcher is trying to keep the research's meaning intact.

#### Central Tension

The researcher is first asked to perform founderhood, then told founderhood may
be missing. This can damage agency and trust if treated as a personal verdict.

#### Diagnosis

The missing object is not motivation and not a single title. It is CEO function
as a bundle.

The current chapter's function list is strong. Rebuild it into a map with three
groups:

- Non-delegable or hard-to-delegate functions often held by the researcher:
  technical meaning, authenticity, why this matters, research-community trust.
- Complementable execution functions: fundraising, hiring, governance,
  contracts, finance, business development, weekly decision cadence.
- Shared/institutional functions: IP, university relationship, disclosure
  sequence, conflict-of-interest process, research continuation.

Do not say every researcher must be CEO. Do not say external CEOs solve the
problem. The point is role architecture.

#### Tool / Reusable Question

Use a CEO-function map:

| Function | Currently carried by | Must stay with researcher? | Can be complemented? | Risk if unassigned |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Reusable question:

> When someone says "CEO", which function are they actually worried about?

#### Bridge To Chapter 5

End by saying that decomposing CEO function is necessary but not sufficient.
Once a project is being pitched, discussed with investors, and shown to
companies, the next danger is that external conversation outruns disclosure,
trust, and IP design. This opens Chapter 5.

### Chapter 5: Before Disclosure

#### Opening Scene

Start from the aftermath of Chapter 4:

- The pitch is now sharper.
- More people want to hear about the technology.
- Company interviews, investor meetings, support-program slides, university
  explanations, and possible press/pitch materials are being prepared.
- The researcher realizes that the project is being pushed outward faster than
  the disclosure rules and trust agreements have been settled.

This makes disclosure a consequence of external acceleration, not a standalone
compliance topic.

#### Central Tension

External learning needs information. But premature or poorly sequenced
disclosure can damage IP, papers, joint research, contracts, and researcher
trust.

The tension should be framed as sequence design:

> How do we learn from the outside world without letting the outside world
> define the project before the inside is protected?

#### Diagnosis

Disclosure is not courage versus caution. It is scope, audience, order, and
consent.

The chapter should show that "protect what matters" includes:

- invention/IP;
- paper/publication plans;
- joint-research boundaries;
- company-candidate confidentiality;
- researcher consent and trust;
- distinction between facts, hypotheses, and support-team interpretation.

#### Tool / Reusable Question

Use a pre-disclosure review:

- What is already public?
- What can be shared under NDA or limited meeting context?
- What must not yet be shared?
- Who has consented?
- Which document will travel fastest?
- Which claim is fact, and which is hypothesis?

Reusable question:

> What must be protected before this conversation creates expectations outside
> the room?

#### Bridge To Chapter 6

End by saying that once functions and disclosure are mapped, the project still
faces one irreversible question: when does it become a company, and what does
that timing make real? This opens Chapter 6.

- The layers of enterprise hearing disclosure.

### Chapter 6: Incorporation Timing

#### Opening Scene

Start with a project after Chapters 4 and 5:

- CEO functions are partly mapped but not fully filled.
- Disclosure scope is partially defined.
- A VC conversation is possible, but DD and fundraising could take 6 to 9
  months.
- Incorporation would create a legal vehicle, but also fixed costs, governance,
  accounting, contracts, expectations, and time pressure.

Use the Stapa transcript insight that first deep-tech fundraising/DD can take
months, and that pre-incorporation work can include VC conversation, IP license
negotiation, share design, registration items, and management preparation. Do
not cite the event.

#### Central Tension

Incorporation is both enabling and hardening:

- Too early: the project inherits fixed costs and expectations before unresolved
  conditions are ready.
- Too late: customer, capital, executive talent, policy timing, and institutional
  energy may move away.

The tension is not "go fast or be cautious." It is conditional timing.

#### Diagnosis

Company formation should be treated as a commitment that makes certain
expectations irreversible. The right question is not "do we have enthusiasm?"
but "which conditions must be true before this legal and social step helps more
than it hardens?"

#### Tool / Reusable Question

Keep GO / WAIT / HOLD / NO_GO / RESOURCE_SHIFT, but connect each word to the
case:

- GO: enough conditions are present to carry the next irreversible step.
- WAIT: active readiness-building before the step.
- HOLD: information missing; define the next evidence task.
- NO_GO: this form should not become a company.
- RESOURCE_SHIFT: move effort toward the weakest readiness area.

Reusable question:

> If we incorporate now, which unresolved condition becomes more expensive,
> more public, or harder to renegotiate?

#### Bridge To Chapter 7

End by saying the next part turns these timing words into a first Before Zero
conversation: how to ask whether the project should become a company now,
later, or not in this form.

#### Use From Current Text

Keep:

- Company formation as a device that creates external expectations and fixed
  responsibilities.
- The early/late incorporation contrast.
- GO / WAIT / HOLD / NO_GO / RESOURCE_SHIFT.
- WAIT as an active decision with return conditions.
- The condition list: technology, customer, IP, policy/social timing, human
  resources, founder function, institutional support.

Move / Compress:

- Generic "会社化が生むもの" can be tighter if the opening scene already makes it
  vivid.

Expand:

- Add the 6-to-9-month VC DD/fundraising time-pressure scene.
- Add pre-incorporation work: VC conversations, IP/license negotiation,

- Tie the timing decision back to unresolved CEO functions from Chapter 4 and

## 5. GAP/VC/CEO Case Zero Design

### 5.1 What To Show In The Prologue

Show only the contradiction:

- A researcher is asked to speak like a founder.
- Later, the project is told investors will worry unless management is
  strengthened or an external executive appears.
- The researcher experiences confusion.

Do not yet explain CEO-function decomposition. The prologue should leave a
question in the reader:

> How can the same system ask one person to step forward and step aside?

### 5.2 How Chapters 1-3 Build The Conditions

Chapter 1 builds the false acceleration condition:

- Research quality and external interest create apparent progress.
- Support adoption and pitch preparation make the project look company-like.
- Company readiness remains unproven.

Chapter 2 builds the clock mismatch condition:

- Program, university, company, VC, and researcher are each rational.
- Each actor's timeline turns the same milestone into a different signal.

Chapter 3 builds the isolation condition:

- Support actors increase, but no one integrates responsibility.
- The researcher is asked to change role depending on room.
- The final unresolved question is: what exactly is the researcher carrying?

### 5.3 How Chapter 4 Explodes The Case

Chapter 4 should be the full explosion:

1. The support program room rewards startup-like performance.
2. The deck and story make the project look closer to company formation.
3. The VC-facing room asks about real execution capacity.
4. "External CEO" appears as a possible solution.
5. The researcher feels the role contradiction.
6. The chapter refuses both crude answers:
   - "researcher must be CEO";
   - "external CEO solves it."
7. The chapter introduces CEO-function decomposition.
8. The tool turns the contradiction into a map.

The emotional motion should be:

- confusion;
- discomfort;
- relief;
- agency.

The reader should feel that the problem can be named without humiliating the
researcher or blaming the VC/support program.

### 5.4 How The Case Returns Later

The case zero should return as a diagnostic example, not as repeated anecdote.

Chapter 8 return:

- Use the same case to fill a role/function worksheet.
- Ask who carries technical meaning, customer development, fundraising,
  governance, university relationship, and weekly execution.

Chapter 9 return:

- Use the same case to ask what must be proven before risk capital.
- Investor feedback becomes a readiness diagnosis, not a verdict.

Chapter 13 return:

- Formalize the founder-readiness distinction.
- Researcher authenticity and technical meaning-making connect to
  non-delegable or hard-to-delegate founder functions.
- Execution capability can be complemented through COO, CEO, venture builder,
  or support-team functions.

Chapter 14 return:

- Ask what institutional pipeline would have prevented the contradiction from
  landing on the researcher alone.
- Connect individual venture readiness to institution readiness: a strong
  institution does not merely add programs; it connects programs into a
  responsibility pipeline.
