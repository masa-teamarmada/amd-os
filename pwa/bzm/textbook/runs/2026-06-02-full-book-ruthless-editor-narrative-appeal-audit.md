## Executive verdict

This manuscript is no longer embarrassing as an internal memo. It is still not
irresistible as a book.

The draft has made a real jump: there is now a repeating composite case, a
stronger public voice, less company-promo leakage, a young commercialization
lead, a researcher, a deck sentence, budget-owner blanks, disclosure colors,
WAIT, RESOURCE_SHIFT, and institutional echo. That is the correct raw
architecture.

But the reading experience is still too obedient. It behaves like a sequence of
well-written lessons wearing scenes as openings. The reader is rarely forced to
turn the page because someone they care about has made a costly choice, hidden a
fear, lost trust, or walked into the next room with unresolved consequences.

Cold verdict:

- As a practical editorial workbook: usable and often intelligent.
- As a public trade manuscript someone keeps reading at night: not yet.
- As a full book with sufficient content: broad, but still thin in lived
  know-how, concrete operational texture, and character consequence.
- As a next-step plan: do not do only a hidden-list surgical pass. Run a P0
  narrative rewrite that deepens the opening, protagonist arc, and act-level
  obligations, while a parallel source-mining worker fills the missing field
  particulars.

The previous "surgical pass is enough" gate is now stale against the current
reader standard. The manuscript may be structurally coherent, but it is not yet
compulsively readable.

## Why this is still not irresistible

The manuscript understands the problem better than it dramatizes it.

The recurring case is present, but it is too clean. The young commercialization
lead makes mistakes, but the mistakes do not cost enough. The researcher grows
quiet, but we do not know enough about what is being threatened inside that
quiet. The support program creates pressure, but its tradeoffs are still mostly
explained. The university protects trust, but the actual institutional friction
is too generalized. The investor asks hard questions, but the room recovers
quickly into useful learning. The external CEO candidate smooths language, but
the reader does not yet feel the near-betrayal.

The book still lets the narrator rescue every chapter.

Too often, the pattern is:

1. Open with a recognizable scene.
2. Explain the structural issue correctly.
3. Name the useful distinction.
4. Close with the next topic.

That is clean instructional prose. It is not narrative compulsion.

An irresistible version would make the reader experience:

- the young lead wanting the program to succeed and therefore strengthening the
  wrong sentence;
- the researcher choosing silence because open objection would make them look
  uncooperative;
- the university officer knowing they are about to be blamed for slowing the
  project, while still needing to stop a disclosure mistake;
- the support manager needing a stronger story because their own program must
  show outcomes;
- the investor seeing a missing budget owner and forcing everyone to admit the
  company clock is premature;
- the external CEO candidate hearing "do not take my research away from me" and
  not yet knowing how to answer.

Right now the book tells us these pressures exist. It does not make us dread the
next meeting.

The content is also not yet thick enough. We have concepts and tools, but not
enough concrete artifacts: actual-looking email language, meeting agendas, red
line comments, investor question sequences, budget-owner discovery paths,
university approval choke points, first paid pilot options, failure-review
formats, or institution pilot operating details. The reader learns what to care
about, but not always how the field actually sounds when it goes wrong.

## Scene density audit

The manuscript has scenes, but many are "scene openings" rather than "scene
engines."

Passable scene engines:

- Ch00: applause and the too-strong sentence.
- Ch01: "interesting" meeting and budget-owner silence.
- Ch05: send reservation stopped with 17 minutes left.
- Ch07: WAIT nearly drifting back to GO.
- Ch09: investor-prep night and budget email.
- Ch15: public-grant sentence weakened under pressure.
- Ch18: repeat-back test.
- Ch19: RESOURCE_SHIFT and paid-option split.
- Ch21: institution meeting without the researcher.

Weak or underdeveloped scene engines:

- Ch02 has email pressure but not enough decisive tradeoff.
- Ch03 has room-hopping but not enough character-level consequence.
- Ch04 has emotional hinge but resolves into function split too quickly.
- Ch06 has good debate but needs a concrete paid-signal story.
- Ch08 has responsibility lists, not enough authority conflict.
- Ch10 has repair logic, but insufficient unresolved damage.
- Ch11 repeats macro pressure without making the opportunity costly enough.
- Ch12-Ch17 drift toward "meeting plus explanation."
- Ch20-Ch24 drift toward "tool plus rationale."

Scene-density rule for next draft:

Every main narrative chapter 00-21 must have:

1. A named room, message, document, meeting, or deadline.
2. A character who wants something now.
3. A second character whose rational need conflicts with it.
4. A document sentence, email, memo field, or spoken line that changes.
5. A cost if the change is wrong.
6. An unresolved question that pulls the next chapter.

If a chapter cannot meet this, it belongs in Field Toolkit, methodology, or
appendix.
