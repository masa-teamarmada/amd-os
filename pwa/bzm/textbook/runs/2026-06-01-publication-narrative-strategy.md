# Publication Narrative Strategy

> Date: 2026-06-01 JST
>
> Scope: `pwa/bzm/public-manuscript/00` to `14`, existing publication
> strategy/positioning notes, and Stapa transcript source material.
>
> This is an internal editorial strategy memo. It is not reader-facing copy.

## 1. Commander Diagnosis

The current public manuscript has cleared the first important gate: it is no
longer a company brochure, it avoids internal operations language, and it
mostly speaks to researchers, URA, industry-collaboration staff, support teams,
and young venture builders.

The next problem is not terminology. It is narrative.

The current `00` to `14` chapters often read as strong standalone explanations.
Each chapter is sensible, but the book does not yet create enough forward
pressure from one chapter to the next. The reader learns many correct lenses:
different clocks, support-program local optimization, disclosure order,
CEO-function decomposition, incorporation timing, risk-capital readiness, XRL,
FRL, ERS. But the manuscript still needs a stronger through-line: a sequence of
field scenes that makes the reader feel why each lens becomes necessary.

The most important scene is the GAP fund / VC / CEO-function contradiction.
It is too large to be used as a quick example in the prologue or a single
explanatory chapter. It should become the book's first major dramatic hinge:

- a researcher is first asked to perform like an entrepreneur;
- the same researcher is later told that an external manager may be needed;
- every actor is locally rational;
- the researcher experiences the whole system as contradictory;
- the missing object is not motivation, but an unassigned CEO function.

That scene can carry the book from field confusion into the later BZM theory.

## 2. Current Manuscript Review

### 00 Prologue

Strength:

- The prologue already promises a reader-first book and defines Before Zero
  clearly.
- It introduces research strength, customer, IP, founder function, capital,
  institution, and timing as a connected problem.

Risk:

- It currently names many important themes before the reader has lived through
  one scene.
- The GAP/VC/CEO contradiction appears too early as one example among many,
  even though it should be the first major narrative event.

Editorial move:

- Open with one composite field scene, not a conceptual overview.
- Use the prologue to make the reader ask: "How can every support actor be
  right and still leave the researcher alone?"
- End by promising that the book will return to that scene and make it legible.

### 01 Research Results Are Not Companies

Strength:

- Strong distinction between research excellence and company readiness.
- Good practical warnings around customer validation, support-program adoption,
  and researcher burden.

Risk:

- The chapter explains the category distinction well, but it does not yet
  create enough friction with a memorable protagonist or situation.
- It can feel like a correct essay rather than the first turn of a narrative.

Editorial move:

- Start from a promising research result entering a support pipeline.
- Let the reader feel the false acceleration: paper, patent, company interest,
  support adoption, pitch deck.
- Close by asking which readiness is actually missing, then hand to Chapter 2.

### 02 Different Clocks

Strength:

- The "clock" metaphor is clear and useful.
- It covers researcher, university, company, investor, and public-program
  rationalities without blaming one actor.

Risk:

- The chapter currently enumerates actor clocks in parallel. It needs a central
  scene where the clocks collide.
- The bridge from "different clocks" to "support can isolate researchers" can
  be stronger.

Editorial move:

- Use the same composite case from Chapter 1 and show how each actor reads the
  same milestone differently.
- End with the reader seeing why clock mismatch becomes loneliness, not just
  delay.

### 03 Support Can Isolate Researchers

Strength:

- This is one of the most important public chapters.
- It correctly says support can increase when integration decreases.

Risk:

- It still behaves partly like a diagnosis chapter. It needs to become the
  emotional center of Part 1.
- The researcher loneliness line should not remain abstract; it should be
  dramatized through concrete moments.

Editorial move:

- Build toward the GAP/VC/CEO contradiction rather than fully consuming it.
- Show the researcher receiving different role demands in different rooms.
- End with the unresolved question: "What exactly is the researcher being asked
  to carry?"

### 04 Before Disclosure

Strength:

- The chapter is practical and field-safe.
- It captures IP, papers, joint research, trust, enterprise interviews, investor
  meetings, and pitch materials.

Risk:

- In the current order, it interrupts the emotional build from support
  isolation to CEO-function contradiction.
- It can read like a compliance/advice chapter unless tied to a scene.

Editorial move:

- Move after the GAP/VC/CEO chapter, or rewrite it as the second consequence of
  the same scene: once the researcher is pushed outward, what must be protected?
- Keep the chapter, but make every section answer "what breaks if the external

### 05 GAP / VC / CEO Function

Strength:

Risk:

## 3. Narrative Strategy

### 3.1 Opening Scene

The book should first take readers into a composite scene like this:

> A researcher with a promising technology enters a support program. The pitch
> gets sharper. The researcher is asked to speak like a founder. The room
> applauds the social impact and market potential. Weeks later, in a financing
> conversation, the same project is told that investors will worry unless an
> external executive is brought in. Nobody intends harm. The program, the VC,
> the university, and the support team each have a rational reason. But the
> researcher is left asking: "Am I supposed to be the entrepreneur, or am I the
> technical asset someone else will manage?"

This scene should not name a specific event or company. It should be written as
an anonymized composite. It should appear lightly in the prologue, build through
Chapters 1 to 3, become the central event in the promoted Chapter 4, and return as the
diagnostic example in Chapters 8, 9, 13, and 14.

### 3.2 Reader Emotional Arc

The reader should move through five emotional states:

1. Recognition: "I have seen this confusion."
2. Discomfort: "The support system may be locally correct but structurally
   incoherent."
3. Relief: "The problem is not that one person is bad; the missing functions
   can be named."
4. Agency: "I can run a better conversation before incorporation."
5. Structure: "The later BZM variables are names for field conditions I already
   understand."

Current manuscript risk:

- Chapters often go directly from diagnosis to questions.
- The reader is informed, but not always pulled.

Required editorial correction:

- Each chapter must change the reader's emotional state, not only add a lens.

### 3.3 Main Through-Line

The book's through-line should be:

1. A strong research result enters a support ecosystem.
2. Different actors read it with different clocks.
3. More support can still leave the researcher alone.
4. The GAP/VC/CEO contradiction reveals that the missing object is a function,
   not a title.
5. Disclosure, incorporation, capital, and roles must be sequenced before the
   company becomes irreversible.
6. Failures become learning when they are recorded as missed conditions, not
   blame.
7. Those field conditions become formal variables: macro timing, XRL, FRL, ERS,
   and bottleneck diagnosis.

## 5. GAP / VC / CEO Treatment

This issue should be handled across the book, not trapped in one chapter.

### Prologue

Use it as a short unanswered scene. Do not explain everything.

Purpose:

- Hook the reader.
- Show contradiction without resolving it.

### Chapters 1 to 3

Build the conditions:

- Chapter 1: research excellence does not equal company readiness.
- Chapter 2: each actor has a different clock.
- Chapter 3: support increases, but integrated responsibility does not.

Purpose:

- By the time Chapter 4 begins, the reader should understand why the
  contradiction happens.

### Chapter 4

Make it the central scene.

Required beats:

1. GAP fund asks the researcher to pitch as a startup founder.
2. The pitch becomes sharper and more public.
3. VC asks who will actually run the company.
4. External CEO suggestion appears.
5. Researcher experiences the demand as a role contradiction.
6. Diagnosis: CEO is not one title; it is a bundle of functions.
7. Tool: CEO-function map.
8. Bridge: founder readiness later formalizes this distinction.

### Chapters 8, 9, 13, 14

Return to the scene from different angles:

- Chapter 8: who carries each function?
- Chapter 9: what should be proven before risk capital?
- Chapter 13: which founder functions are non-delegable and which can be
  complemented?
- Chapter 14: what institutional pipeline would prevent this contradiction
  from landing on the researcher alone?

### Researcher

Entry emotion:

- "I am being asked to become something, but nobody has explained what."

What the book must give:

- Permission to separate research authenticity from full CEO burden.
- A language for what to keep, what to share, and what others must carry.

### URA / Industry-Collaboration Staff / TLO

Entry emotion:

- "We support researchers, but the support sometimes fragments."

What the book must give:

- A way to integrate support programs, IP, disclosure, company timing, and
  founder-function conversations.

### Young Venture Builder / CEO Candidate / EIR

Entry emotion:

- "I want to help, but I do not know where I fit before the company exists."

What the book must give:

- A map of functions they can carry without taking the researcher's center away.

### VC / CVC / Grant Operator

Entry emotion:

- "We ask reasonable questions, but those questions may land as role confusion."

What the book must give:

- A way to treat feedback as readiness diagnosis rather than a verdict.

## 7. Book-Level Writing Rules

### 7.1 Chapter Pattern

Every chapter should follow this pattern:

1. Scene: one concrete composite situation.
2. Tension: what feels contradictory or unfair from the field.
3. Diagnosis: what structure explains the tension.
4. Tool / Question: what the reader can do next.
5. Bridge: how this chapter changes the next chapter or later theory.

This pattern does not need visible headings in the final manuscript. It is an
editorial skeleton.

### 7.2 No More Heading Lists

Small headings are allowed only if each heading advances the chapter's central
tension.

Editorial test:

- If a section can be moved to another chapter without loss, it is probably an
  explanation block, not a narrative step.
- If a section introduces a new lens but does not change what the reader feels
  or can do, it should be shortened or moved to a tool appendix.

### 7.3 Big Field Insight Rule

A field insight must not be consumed in one sentence if it meets any of these
conditions:

- it involves three or more actors;
- it changes how a researcher sees their role;
- it affects incorporation, capital, IP, or founder function;
- it becomes a later BZM variable;
- it can make a reader feel seen, accused, or relieved.

For such insights, require at least:

1. a scene;
2. actor-by-actor rationality;
3. the contradiction as felt by the person in the middle;
4. a structural diagnosis;
5. a reusable question or tool;
6. a later theory bridge.

The GAP/VC/CEO contradiction clearly passes this threshold.

### 7.4 Bridge Rule

Every chapter ending must do two jobs:

- give one usable reader action;
- create a reason to read the next chapter.

The current "明日使える問い" sections are useful, but they should not be the
only ending. Add a final bridge paragraph that says what remains unresolved.

Examples:

- Chapter 3 should end by asking what the researcher is being made to carry,
  which opens Chapter 4.
- Chapter 4 should end by saying function decomposition is still not enough
  unless external conversations are sequenced safely, which opens disclosure.
- Chapter 10 should end by saying repeated field misses become model variables,
  which opens Chapter 11.

### 7.5 Anonymous Case Rule

Use composite anonymous cases as the default public evidence form.

Case details should include:

- role type, not real organization name;
- one concrete field moment;
- the contradiction or missed signal;
- the revised question.

Avoid:

- private event names;
- company-centered proof;
- identifiable live project details;
- internal system/process vocabulary.

### 7.6 Theory Ratio

For Chapters 1 to 14:

- 80 percent field language;
- 15 percent practical tool/question;
- 5 percent theory bridge.

Formal names can appear near the bridge, but the reader should understand the
human problem before seeing the variable.
