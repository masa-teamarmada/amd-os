# The Before Zero Model: measuring deep-tech ventures and their institutional nurseries before day zero

**Abstract** (S4 で最終化 — outline 版を仮置き): Deep-tech ventures born from universities accumulate — or fail to accumulate — most of their value before incorporation, yet this "Before Zero" stage has no measurement theory. We propose the Before Zero Model (BZM): a two-layer observation system with a multiplicative venture ledger (PRS) and a weighted-sum institutional ledger (ERS), linked by a Triple-Helix macro state (σ_SU). We prove the two ledgers cannot be merged: defining institution-adjusted value as the venture's expected value under its institution's nurturing dynamics, the stage-contingent operability of institutional capabilities defeats every monotone single-score merger (an impossibility in the Arrow mold), and we register falsifiable statistical signatures of the error committed by "institution-adjusted" composite rankings. A weighted sum is shown to be the unique admissible institutional aggregate, and the go/wait decision emerges as the optimal-stopping rule operating on the two ledgers. A retrospective calibration on eight deep-tech projects (2007–2026) illustrates the system. We close by registering a falsification program for Before Zero Studies.

**Keywords**: deep-tech ventures; university spin-outs; readiness measurement; technology readiness levels; Triple Helix; impossibility theorem; real options; research commercialization

## 1. Introduction

A national screening panel convenes to rank some forty university deep-tech seeds competing for a commercialization fund.¹ Its instrument is an "institution-adjusted project score": each seed's evaluated quality, multiplied by a coefficient reflecting its host university's technology-transfer capabilities. Midway through the session a member proposes re-weighting the institutional coefficient; two seeds swap ranks; a debate follows about which weighting is *correct*. The debate assumes its own answer — that some weighting is correct, that the committee's task is calibration. This paper proves the assumption false. Under requirements every member of that committee would separately endorse — a dead venture is worth zero anywhere; a better institution never hurts — together with two facts of technology-transfer practice — institutional capabilities bind at different venture stages, and act over the venture's remaining journey — *no* institution-adjusted single score exists: not the current weighting, not the proposed one, not any monotone combination however sophisticated. The committee is not miscalibrating an instrument; it is calibrating a nonexistent one.

The setting is the *pre-founding* stage of deep-tech venturing, which we call **Before Zero**: the years — typically five to fifteen — during which a research result inside a university or national laboratory matures toward incorporation ("day zero"). Along that road the decisive assets accumulate: technology readiness, intellectual property, regulatory position, founding team, institutional scaffolding (Shane, 2004; Grimaldi et al., 2011; Perkmann et al., 2013). By day zero, much of a deep-tech venture's fate is already committed. Yet the stage is a measurement vacuum: too early for valuation — no cash flows to discount, no comparables to multiply — it is governed in practice by readiness vocabularies (Mankins, 1995) and scorecards, and increasingly by policy demands for composite rankings of seeds, institutions, and ecosystems (Nardo et al., 2008).

This paper proposes a measurement theory for that vacuum: the **Before Zero Model (BZM)**. Its architecture is deliberately dual — two ledgers with different mathematical shapes. The *venture ledger* scores each project multiplicatively, $\mathrm{PRS} = P \cdot R \cdot S$ (potential scale × realization readiness × survival probability), because a pre-founding venture is a conjunction of necessary conditions in which any zero is fatal. The *institutional ledger* scores the hosting nursery additively, $\mathrm{ERS}$, a weighted sum over eight rubric-scored capability axes, because an institution is a portfolio of partially substitutable capabilities whose gaps must stay visible. A shared macro state $\sigma_{SU}$ — the shifted geometric mean of academic, industrial, and governmental momenta, Triple Helix in conception (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000) — drives the venture ledger's dynamics.

The central result explains why the architecture *must* be dual. We define the institution-adjusted value of a seed honestly — as the expected discounted value of the venture under its institution's nurturing dynamics, the same object the decision layer of Section 5 operates on — so that a dead venture is worth zero anywhere (C1′), a better nursery never hurts (C2′), institutions act on value only through the venture's state dynamics (C3′, the class's defining channel), and value moves continuously (C4′). **Theorem 3** establishes that once institutional capabilities are stage-contingent in their operability — scouting and incubation bind early in the realization journey, licensing and the corporate interface bind late — no single-score representation $\Phi(g(P,R,S), h(A))$ monotone in its indices can represent that value: the true comparison of two nurseries reverses across venture strata, and a single institutional index cannot lie on both sides of a reversal. The composites found in practice die in two distinct ways: pure addition ranks corpses above live ventures at the annihilation boundary, while pure multiplication and every monotone blend — however sophisticated the weighting — fall to the stage-contingency reversal (multiplication, in addition, double-counts a channel that already acts inside the venture's trajectory). **Corollary 3.1** converts the impossibility into empirics: a field that ranks by single scores anyway will leave *statistical scars* — Simpson reversals of institution coefficients across venture-quality quartiles, quartile instability, Hausman divergence between pooled and conditional estimators — stated here as pre-registrable hypotheses rather than rhetoric.

Two further results complete the system. **Theorem 1** shows the weighted sum is not a convenient choice but the *unique* institutional aggregate — in rubric units, given the rubric's equal-interval reporting convention — admissible under five practice-grounded axioms (separability, compensability, audited missing-visibility, monotonicity, cross-context invariance), by additive conjoint measurement (Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky, 1971); a precondition gate for formal bylaws (Proposition 1) sharpens it in the spirit of Alkire and Foster (2011). **Theorem 4** then shows the system is operable: the go/wait decision emerges as an optimal-stopping rule on the two ledgers (Dixit and Pindyck, 1994), with an *endogenous* threshold $\theta_\sigma^*$ — how much macro tailwind suffices is derived from project economics ($P$, burn, slack, regime credibility), not asserted — and comparative statics that map policy instruments to distinct arguments of the threshold. We order the contributions deliberately: measurement first; the decision operator demonstrates the measurement's completeness rather than headlining the paper.

We exercise the system on the operating records of a single venture studio: eight deep-tech projects across Japanese universities and national laboratories, 2007–2026, reported as composites with type-named institutions. The exercise is retrospective calibration under explicit Tier discipline — outcomes were known when rubrics were scored, so it demonstrates organization of history, not prediction. Its two vignettes are nonetheless instructive: a macro-blessed, technology-hollow project that any composite would have founded (and history did found, into a decade of stagnation), and a technically presentable project whose potential-scale zero the multiplicative ledger catches while additive scores rank it above the first. The genuinely predictive test — prospective, pre-registered, multiplicity-corrected — is specified as a research program rather than claimed as an accomplishment.

Three contributions, then, to this journal's readership. *A measurement theory for an unmeasured stage*: axiomatic foundations for evaluating ventures that do not yet exist, where valuation is structurally silent. *An impossibility as policy guardrail*: proof that the single-score instrument evaluation practice keeps demanding cannot exist, together with what legitimately survives — additive shortlists with public weights, conditional gates, and governance separation of the two ledgers. *A falsification-registered research program* — **Before Zero Studies** — whose central claims are built to be testable and whose violation signatures are specified in advance (Popper, 1959).

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 locates the gap at the intersection of five literatures. Section 3 constructs the two ledgers and proves the institutional aggregate unique. Section 4 states the axioms and the impossibility theorem, with its falsifiable corollary. Section 5 derives the GO operator and its comparative statics. Section 6 reports the retrospective calibration. Section 7 draws design implications and registers the program; Section 8 concludes. Scope is declared at the outset: deep-tech ventures, Japanese university and national-laboratory context, pre-founding stage. Whether the theory travels is an empirical question the program is built to answer, not a claim we make.

## 2. Two evaluation problems, one field

Readiness scales are the pre-founding evaluator's incumbent technology. Technology Readiness Levels, born as a NASA communication protocol for hardware maturity (Mankins, 1995), have diffused far beyond their origin: European framework programs mandate them, national funding agencies gate on them, and the family has multiplied into business, societal, and organizational variants — the European Commission's Societal Readiness Level among them. Their strength is real: a shared ordinal ladder disciplines conversation across communities that otherwise talk past each other. Their limits are equally real and well documented (Olechowski et al., 2020): each scale is a single ordinal axis with no aggregation theory — nothing in the TRL apparatus says how technology maturity should combine with market, team, or institutional maturity into an assessment of the *venture*, and nothing prevents practitioners from averaging ordinal levels as if they were cardinal quantities. Readiness scales, in short, supply vocabulary without measurement theory.

Where multiple axes must become one number, evaluation practice reaches for composite indicators, and here the methodological literature has long sounded alarms. The canonical handbook (Nardo et al., 2008) catalogs the arbitrariness of weighting and normalization choices; systematic reviews document rank reversals under defensible alternative constructions (Greco et al., 2019); and the deepest critique is Sen's (1977): an index that buries heterogeneous dimensions in a single number conceals precisely the trade-offs that deliberation should surface. The constructive exception proves instructive — the Alkire–Foster family of multidimensional poverty measures (Alkire and Foster, 2011) survives scrutiny because it separates *identification* (who is poor) from *aggregation* (how poor overall), axiomatizing each step. Innovation evaluation has imported the composite habit without importing the axiomatic discipline; this paper's Proposition 1 and Theorem 3 are, in one reading, that discipline arriving.

A third literature studies our institutional layer directly. Technology-transfer offices, gap funds, incubators, entrepreneurs-in-residence, and spin-out policies have each generated substantial research programs (Bozeman, 2000; Shane, 2004; Grimaldi et al., 2011; Munari et al., 2016; Hayter et al., 2018), and their collective finding is that institutional capabilities materially shape spin-out formation and survival. Yet this literature measures the institution *separately* from the venture: its dependent variables are counts, rates, and survival curves at the institution or policy level. Evaluation practice, meanwhile, merges the two levels daily — every "university-adjusted" project ranking and every "ecosystem-weighted" seed score is such a merger — and the merger itself has, to our knowledge, never been theorized. The gap this paper fills is not the study of institutions or of ventures but of the *aggregation across levels* that practice performs without a license.

The Triple Helix program (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000) supplies our macro layer's conceptual frame: innovation capacity emerges from the co-evolution of academia, industry, and government, and one strand has made the frame quantitative through mutual-information synergy indicators (Leydesdorff, 2003). What the program lacks is a *venture-facing* state variable — a quantity a founder–institution pair can observe and a stopping rule can condition on. The synergy indicator $T_{AIG}$ diagnoses a national or regional *system*; no decision about a particular seed follows from it. Our $\sigma_{SU}$ is built to occupy exactly this vacant slot — Triple Helix in conception, decision-theoretic in function — and Section 3 is careful to keep the two objects distinct rather than claiming to improve one with the other.

Entry timing under uncertainty is the fifth ingredient, and the real-options literature supplies it in mature form: irreversible investment, option value of waiting, threshold policies (McDonald and Siegel, 1986; Dixit and Pindyck, 1994), with applications to R&D and staged innovation investment (McGrath, 1997). Two features of the standard apparatus fail before day zero. The monitored state is a firm-level cash flow or output price — observable for going concerns, nonexistent for pre-founding ventures. And the threshold, however elegantly derived, floats free of any *measurement system*: the literature says "invest when the state crosses the bar" without saying how the state is to be constructed for an entity that does not yet exist. Section 5 marries the stopping machinery to the measurement layer of Section 3, which is, we believe, the first such marriage in this domain.

Finally, the tools of this paper's central result come from a tradition innovation studies has rarely visited: the measurement-theoretic and social-choice lineage. Arrow (1963) established the template — reasonable axioms, jointly unsatisfiable — for preference aggregation; Debreu (1960) and the conjoint-measurement school (Luce and Tukey, 1964; Krantz et al., 1971; Wakker, 1989) characterized exactly when multi-attribute orderings admit additive or separable representations, with sign-consistency and the Thomsen/double-cancellation condition as the load-bearing tests. These results are sixty years old, rigorous, and almost entirely absent from the evaluation practices they indict. Importing them is this paper's methodological move: Theorem 1 applies Krantz–Luce–Suppes–Tversky machinery to an institutional object those authors never considered, and the impossibility of Section 4 rebuilds Arrow's template — individually reasonable requirements, a richness condition on the admissible domain, joint unsatisfiability — at the venture–institution interface, where the richness is supplied not by preference profiles but by how nurseries act on seeds.

The synthesis, then: five literatures each hold one piece — vocabulary (readiness scales), aggregation warnings (composite indicators), the institutional layer (technology transfer), the macro frame (Triple Helix), and timing (real options) — and none holds the piece that connects them: a measurement theory for the pre-founding venture that respects venture multiplicativity, derives institutional additivity, proves the two layers cannot be merged, and hands the result to a stopping rule. The Before Zero Model is our attempt to build that connective theory, and everything in it is constructed so that its central claims can be wrong in public: the axioms are stated for inspection, and the impossibility's violation signatures are registered for detection.

## 3. The two-layer observation system

Consider a research seed $i$ hosted by an institution $j$ — a university, a national laboratory, or a public research organization — at time $t$ before incorporation. What can actually be observed? Not cash flows, market shares, or churn: none of these exist yet. What can be observed are rubric-scored states: the maturity of the technology and of the surrounding preparations, the institution's own capabilities (its bylaws, licensing practice, gap funding, entrepreneurial staffing), and the macro environment in which academia, industry, and government pay attention to the seed's domain. The Before Zero measurement problem is to organize these observables so that they support decisions — without pretending to a precision that the stage cannot deliver. Our answer is deliberately dual: two ledgers with different mathematical shapes, plus one macro state variable they share.

The **venture ledger** assigns to each project the triple $(P, R, S)$ and the score
$$\mathrm{PRS}_{i}(t) = P_i(t) \cdot R_i(t) \cdot S_i(t),$$
where $P$ is the *potential scale* of the opportunity conditional on full realization, $R$ is *realization readiness* — how far the technology, team, and commercialization preparations have actually progressed toward that potential — and $S$ is the *survival probability* that the venture-to-be endures long enough to complete the journey. The multiplicative form is not a stylistic choice. A pre-founding venture is a conjunction of necessary conditions, and production processes whose success requires every link to hold are weakest-link economies in the sense of the O-ring literature (Kremer, 1993): excellence in two factors cannot rescue a zero in the third. A seed with world-class science but a market of zero economic size, or a vast market but a technology that cannot leave the laboratory, is not "average"; it is dead. Multiplication, unlike any averaging rule, encodes this mortality.

The two ledgers share one **macro state**. Let $\mu_A$, $\mu_I$, and $\mu_G$ denote the momenta of academic, industrial, and governmental attention to the seed's domain — publication dynamics, industrial commitment, and policy budgets, each observed as a time series. Define
$$\sigma_{SU}(t) = \sqrt[3]{(\mu_A + 1)(\mu_I + 1)(\mu_G + 1)} - 1,$$
the shifted geometric mean of the three momenta. Conceptually this is a Triple Helix object (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000): the claim embedded in the geometric-mean form — an equal-weight Cobb–Douglas aggregate of the shifted momenta (Cobb and Douglas, 1928) — is that macro tailwind is a *joint* property of the three helices — government money without industrial pull, or academic fashion without policy commitment, does not constitute alignment. We stress that $\sigma_{SU}$ is not Leydesdorff's (2003) mutual-information indicator $T_{AIG}$: the two measure different things (a venture-facing state variable versus a system-level synergy diagnostic), respond to different perturbations, and serve different decision problems; a formal cross-walk is developed in the companion monograph. In what follows, $\sigma_{SU}$ evolves through discrete regimes (dormant $S_0$, warming $S_1$, aligned $S_2$), and the venture ledger's dynamics — in particular the drift of $R$ and the hazard structure behind $S$ — condition on it.

The **institutional ledger** observes the nursery rather than the seed. Institution $j$ is scored on eight axes $A_1, \ldots, A_8$ (each on a 0–4 rubric; the full rubric is summarized in Supplementary Material D): (1) seed scouting and technology assessment; (2) IP management and licensing (TLO function); (3) incubation and entrepreneurship support; (4) industry-collaboration and corporate interface; (5) funding access, including gap funds; (6) entrepreneurial management talent (EIR/CXO supply); (7) entrepreneurship bylaws and governance; and (8) government and policy linkage. Five axioms discipline how these observations may be aggregated. **E1 (separability)**: the contribution of one axis to institutional readiness can be assessed without knowing the level of every other axis. **E2 (compensability)**: institutional capabilities partially substitute — a thin incubation program can be compensated, at some rate, by a strong industry interface or external fund access; no axis *other than the bylaws precondition* (axis 7, whose special status Proposition 1 formalizes) is individually fatal to the *institution* (unlike the venture's factors, where any zero kills). **E3 (audited missing-visibility)**: an unobserved axis must remain visible as missing rather than silently imputed — "unknown" and "not started" are different states (Rubin, 1976) — and the observed set is fixed by the assessor's evidence standard: an institution that *declines to evidence* an axis is scored at the rubric floor rather than treated as unobserved, so opacity cannot raise a score (SM-A develops the anti-gaming protocol). **E4 (monotonicity)**: improving any observed axis raises institutional readiness — no axis is inert. **E5 (cross-context invariance)**: the rate at which two observed capabilities trade against each other does not depend on which further axes happen to be observed — an assessor who has calibrated how gap-fund access trades against incubation capacity does not recalibrate that judgment because the bylaws axis was unobservable at one institution. Each axiom is a statement about technology-transfer practice before it is mathematics: committees do assess bylaws without auditing the incubator, networks do substitute for in-house capacity, and honest gap diagnosis requires that ignorance stay visible without rewarding it.

**Theorem 1** (additive representation; proof in SM-A). *Let institutional readiness be a family of continuous orderings, one for each observed set $K_{obs} \subseteq \{1,\ldots,8\}$, satisfying E1–E5 together with the standard conjoint-measurement solvability and Archimedean conditions and the Thomsen/double-cancellation property (on the continuum idealization of the rubric stated in SM-A), and let the rubric carry the equal-interval anchor convention (E2-s). Then a single weight vector $w_1, \ldots, w_8 > 0$ serves every observed set, and each ordering is representable as*
$$\mathrm{ERS}_j = 100 \cdot \frac{\sum_{k \in K_{obs}} w_k A_{jk}}{4 \sum_{k \in K_{obs}} w_k},$$
*unique up to positive affine transformation among scores additive in rubric units.* The result is an application of additive conjoint measurement (Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky, 1971, ch. 6; Debreu, 1960; Wakker, 1989), with the cross-context clause a cousin of subgroup consistency in poverty measurement (Foster and Shorrocks, 1991), and its content deserves careful statement: the weighted sum in rubric units is the *unique aggregate consistent with an equal-interval reporting convention* — without E2-s the same axioms deliver a weighted sum of monotone recalibrations of each axis, so what the convention buys is exactly the linearity committees audit. The ratios $w_k/w_{k'}$ are empirical content recovered from observed trade-offs; the paper's default of equal weights is a disclosed working convention pending that elicitation, not a consequence of the axioms. Whereas the venture ledger multiplies because ventures die of their weakest link, the institutional ledger adds because institutions are portfolios of partially substitutable capabilities.

One qualification sharpens the additive form rather than contradicting it. **Proposition 1** (precondition gate). *Axis 7 — entrepreneurship bylaws and formal rules — enters not as an additive term but as a gate: $\mathrm{ERS}_j = 100 \cdot g(A_{j7}) \cdot F(A_{j,-7})$, where $g$ is a step (or steep sigmoid) function and $F$ is the Theorem-1 aggregate over the remaining axes.* An institution scoring 44% on capabilities but with no legal pathway for a professor to found a company has idle capacity, not readiness: every other capability waits on a rule that does not exist. The structure mirrors the identification-then-aggregation discipline of multidimensional poverty measurement (Alkire and Foster, 2011) — first determine whether the institution is *in the game*, then measure how much of the game it has built. Formally, axioms E1–E5 govern the seven non-gate axes, and axis 7 enters through the gate premise alone — E2 is withdrawn for it by design — so the gate refines Theorem 1 rather than contradicting it (the gate's own axioms, and the consistency of the block, are made explicit in SM-A).

Before asking whether the two ledgers can be merged into one number, note what is already prohibited. **Theorem 2** (no multiplicative cross-combination; proof in SM-A). *Any multiplicative cross-combination $\mathrm{ERS}^{\alpha} \cdot \mathrm{PRS}^{1-\alpha}$, $\alpha \in (0,1)$, preserves the institutional ordering at each fixed venture state — but destroys the calibrated reporting scale of the institutional ledger: rubric steps no longer trade at level-independent rates within the institutional layer, and the marginal credit for every institutional axis becomes contingent on the venture state, so the nursery is paid once inside the venture score and again as a multiplier.* The claim is deliberately cardinal: what the merger breaks is the interval-scale report by which committees audit published weights — the property the equal-interval convention delivers in Theorem 1 — and the venture-contingent credit is double counting, whose full value-level form Section 4 proves. Theorem 2 thus disqualifies the most popular composite as a *reporting instrument*; Section 4 asks the general question: is *any* single score possible?

The economic logic behind the shape difference bears stating plainly, because it drives everything that follows. A venture is a *conjunction*: every necessary condition must hold, so the informative question is "what kills this deal?" — and the multiplicative ledger makes the killer visible as the smallest factor. An institution is a *portfolio*: capabilities substitute at the margin, so the informative question is "what should we build next?" — and the additive ledger makes the answer visible as the largest weighted gap. Forcing either object into the other's shape destroys precisely the information the evaluator needs: an additive venture score hides the fatal zero; a multiplicative institution score turns one weak axis into a false death sentence.

Operationalization follows measurement-theoretic discipline. All axis scores are ordinal rubrics (Stevens, 1946) anchored by observable states, not judgment scales; the distinction between "unknown" and "not started" is coded explicitly and propagates to $K_{obs}$ (Little and Rubin, 2019); institutions are reported as anonymized types and projects as composites (Section 6); and the calibration constants that map rubric levels into the model's numerical ranges are treated as a *calibration procedure* — reported as method, not as results — both to respect proprietary operating data and because the theorems of this paper are invariant to them.

## 4. The impossibility theorem

The temptation this section formalizes is ubiquitous. An evaluation committee holds a venture-level score and an institution-level score and wants one number per (project, institution) pair: $f(P, R, S; A) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, an "institution-adjusted project value" by which to rank a national portfolio of seeds, allocate a gap fund, or compare universities' pipelines. The demand is reasonable on its face — decisions are one-dimensional, so why not the measure? We take the demand seriously and make its target precise — and prove the target empty: once two observable facts about how nurseries act on seeds are admitted, no monotone merger of the two ledgers can represent venture value.

The first step is to say what an "institution-adjusted project value" honestly *is*. A live seed's value is not a static attribute of its current readings; it is the expected discounted value of what the venture will become under its institution's care — the same object Section 5 operates on. We therefore define $f(P, R, S; A)$ as the value function of a *nurturing environment*: the venture state evolves under laws of motion whose coefficients — the drift of $R$, the hazard behind $S$, the slack $F$, the exposure to $\sigma_{SU}$ innovations — depend on the institutional profile $A$, and $f$ is the expected discounted payoff under those dynamics (the admissible class, and a deterministic benchmark subclass in which every proof below is elementary calculus, are specified in SM-B). On this definition, three requirements that would otherwise have to be postulated become structural properties. **C1′ (venture annihilation)**: a venture with no market or no survival — $\min(P, S) = 0$ — is absorbed at zero value regardless of $A$: nobody funds a dead project because its university has excellent bylaws. Realization is deliberately *excluded* from the annihilation set: $R = 0$ is the normal state of an early seed, and its value is an option, not a corpse — gap funds exist precisely to fund $R \approx 0$ projects on institution and potential. (A founder-side microfoundation of C1′ is treated in the companion monograph; the property stands on the mortality argument alone.) **C2′ (institutional monotonicity)**: a better nursery never makes a live project less valuable — improving any axis improves drifts and hazards, hence value. **C4′ (continuity)**: value moves continuously in states and profiles. The fourth item is not a derived property but the class's defining restriction. **C3′ (causal channel)**: institutional axes enter *only* the laws of motion of the venture's state — realization, survival, slack, exposure — and carry no direct premium at a fixed venture path. This is a causal statement with empirical content: bylaws, gap funds, and mentors matter because they accelerate realization, improve survival, and buy runway — not because a committee enjoys the institution's company. A brand or halo channel, by which measured value rises in $A$ with the venture's trajectory held fixed, is excluded by the class definition — and is exactly what the generalized-method-of-moments overidentification test registered in Section 7 is built to detect (Hansen, 1982), so the restriction is submitted to data rather than asserted as dogma.

The channel restriction must face, rather than wave at, the strongest empirical objection to it. A substantial literature documents *certification effects*: affiliation with prominent partners raises young ventures' survival and speeds their capital acquisition (Stuart, Hoang and Hybels, 1999); entrepreneurs accept discounted terms to obtain high-reputation venture backing (Hsu, 2004); early-stage public grants causally improve subsequent private financing (Howell, 2017) — and every practitioner in our context knows the folk version, the premium a national-university seal commands. Do these findings break C3′? The class answers with a distinction between *price* and *value*. What certification demonstrably moves is the venture's access to resources: capital arrives sooner, on better terms, from better partners. In the model those are trajectory consequences — endorsement and access operate on the slack $F$, the survival hazard, and the exposure to $\sigma_{SU}$ innovations, carriers *inside* the licensed channel, and SM-D routes exactly the axes that carry certification in practice ($A_4$, $A_5$, $A_8$) through them. What the class excludes is only the residual: a premium on measured value at a *fixed* venture trajectory — a halo that changes what a committee writes down without changing what the venture does or survives. That residual is precisely what the Section 7 overidentification test estimates; certification effects that work through trajectories sit inside its null, not against it. And the theorem degrades gracefully if the test rejects: Theorem 3's proof uses only the reversal condition SH, a directly observable property of value orderings, so a documented direct channel would change the class's interpretation — value partly constituted by perception — without rescuing the single score, unless the direct premium were strong and uniform enough to undo every stage-contingent reversal: itself an empirical question, and one the registered program is built to price.

The theorem needs two further ingredients, and they are facts about nurseries rather than mathematical conveniences. **SH (stage-contingent operability)**: different institutional capabilities bind at different stages of the realization journey. Scouting and incubation act on a seed still finding its shape; licensing execution and the corporate interface act on a seed approaching the market. Consequently the value ranking of two institutional profiles can genuinely *reverse* across venture strata: for an early seed, the incubation-strong nursery is worth more than the licensing-strong one; for a late seed, the reverse (SM-B.3 exhibits the reversal in closed form). **ED (exposure-duration asymmetry)**: whatever an institution does, it does over the venture's *remaining* journey, so institutional strength compounds strongly for an early seed with a long road left and weakly for a seed nearly done. Consequently the ranking of two *ventures* can reverse as their shared institution strengthens (SM-B.3 again). Both conditions are observable, both are standard talk in technology-transfer practice, and both enter the registered program as premises to be tested, not as dogma.

One preliminary result disposes of a class practice does not actually use. Any composite $\Phi(g(P,R,S), h(A))$ with $\Phi$ *strictly* increasing in both arguments is inconsistent with annihilation alone: on the death set $\min(P,S)=0$ every institutional comparison ties at zero, while strict monotonicity in the institutional index forces strict gaps (Lemma SM-B.2). We state this as a lemma, not a theorem, because it is easy — an instance, transported across observation levels, of the familiar fact that veto and annihilation structures defeat strictly monotone separable representations (Fishburn, 1976; Bouyssou and Marchant, 2007) — and because it has no bite against practice: the composites committees actually field are *weakly* monotone, flat in the institution exactly where the venture is dead. Pure multiplication, geometric and CES blends, and min-gates all pass the boundary test. The question that matters is whether any weakly monotone merger survives the two facts of the preceding paragraph.

**Theorem 3** (single-index impossibility; proof in SM-B). *Let venture value be generated by any admissible nurturing environment satisfying SH (respectively ED). Then no functions $g$, $h$ and no merging rule $\Phi$ non-decreasing in its institutional argument (respectively its venture argument) represent the value ordering: $f = \Phi\big(g(P,R,S),\, h(A)\big)$ fails on a pair of live, interior states.* The argument is short enough to state. Under SH there are two live venture states and two institutional profiles whose value comparison reverses across the states. A single score reads the institution through one index $h(A)$: at the early state the representation forces $h(A) > h(A')$, and monotonicity of $\Phi$ then forces the composite to rank $A$ above $A'$ at *every* venture state — including the late state, where the true ranking has reversed. No calibration escapes: nothing is assumed about $g$ and $h$ beyond their existence, so the exclusion covers every weighting a committee might tune — multiplication, weighted addition, geometric means, CES mixes, min-gated and lexicographic hybrids, and every monotone recalibration of any of them. Under ED the same dichotomy runs through the venture index. A composite that is *not* monotone in its own indices defeats the purpose of scoring — no committee fields an instrument on which a better institution score lowers the composite — so the demanded object does not exist. The committee of Section 1 is not miscalibrating an instrument; it is calibrating a nonexistent one.

Two remarks locate the content. First, the dynamics are not decoration. On a static domain, weakly monotone single scores consistent with annihilation and monotonicity *do* exist — $f = \min(\mathrm{PRS}, \kappa(A))$ with $\kappa$ a calibrated institutional index is one — and no axiom system that constrains a static $f$ pointwise can exclude them. What excludes them is stage-contingency of the dynamics: under SH the true institutional comparison lies on both sides of a reversal that any single index must call one way (SM-B.4). The dynamic foundation is the source of the theorem's content, not a technical reformulation. Second, the impossibility bites where committees operate: its witnesses are interior, live states — pre-screened portfolios — not boundary pathologies; the death boundary is needed only for the easy lemma.

The portfolio version supplies the Arrow-style reading. **Corollary 3.2** (portfolio impossibility; SM-B.5). *If the portfolio spans technology domains rich enough that some institutional axis is operative for one seed's environment and inert for another's, then no environment-blind score $F(P,R,S;A)$ — separable or not — represents value across the portfolio.* The proof is immediate, and we flag as much; the content is in the domain condition, exactly as in preference aggregation, where it is the richness of admissible profiles, not any single profile, that defeats the rule (Arrow, 1963). One formula, forty seeds, forty response structures: the formula must misrank somewhere, and Corollary 3.1 says the misranking is statistically visible.

Table 1 organizes the disposal of the three families found in practice. *Pure addition* $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$ dies at the boundary: a dead venture at a strong institution outranks a live venture at a weak one — the ranking rewards the nursery for the corpse (and, being monotone, it falls to Theorem 3 besides). *Pure multiplication* $\mathrm{PRS} \cdot \mathrm{ERS}$ passes the boundary — a zero kills the product — but is a monotone composite, so Theorem 3 applies; it moreover double-counts the institution, whose capabilities already act inside $R$, $S$, and $F$ by C3′, and Theorem 2 excludes it at the ledger level. *General monotone blends* — geometric, CES, min-gated, lexicographic — are Theorem 3's direct quarry. Each family fails for its own reason, which is why practitioners patching one failure reliably create another.

**Table 1 — Three families of single scores and where each fails**

| Family | Practice example | Boundary test (C1′) | Stage-reversal test (SH/ED; Theorem 3) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pure multiplication $\mathrm{PRS} \cdot \mathrm{ERS}$ | "project score × institution coefficient" | passes (flat at death) | **fails** — one institutional index; also double-counts the channel (Theorem 2) | inadmissible |
| Pure addition $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$ | weighted scorecard total | **fails** (dead ventures ranked by their host) | fails | inadmissible as measurement; usable as explicit decision aid with a liveness guard (§7) |
| General monotone $\Phi(g, h)$ | geometric blends, CES mixes, min-gates, lexicographic hybrids | boundary-respecting members are institution-flat at death (Lemma SM-B.2) | **fails** (Theorem 3) | inadmissible |

**Corollary 3.1** (registered diagnostics; SM-B.6). *Suppose a field nonetheless ranks by some single monotone score $\Phi(g, h)$ while outcomes are generated by an admissible environment with operative, stage-contingent channels. Then a constant-coefficient institutional specification is misspecified, and we register three diagnostics: (i) Simpson reversal — the sign of the institution coefficient in a pooled outcome regression differs from its sign within $\mathrm{PRS}$-quartiles (Simpson, 1951); (ii) quartile instability — institution coefficients differ significantly across venture-quality quartiles; (iii) Hausman divergence — pooled and venture-conditional estimators diverge beyond sampling error (Hausman, 1978).* Signature (ii) is the primary registered test: it follows directly from SH, under which the institutional slope genuinely varies across strata (and from ED, under which the institutional increment scales with remaining exposure). Signature (i) requires, in addition, selection of the observed sample on the score itself — only funded or founded seeds are observed — a mechanism the registry protocol specifies; signature (iii) is (ii) in estimator-contrast form. The theorem thus predicts the statistical scars of its own violation, and the signatures are pre-registered in Section 7 with multiplicity-corrected thresholds rather than left as rhetoric.

What survives the impossibility is precisely what committees should build instead. First, *additive shortlisting* $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$ remains available — not as a measurement, which C1′ forbids it to be, but as an explicit decision aid whose trade-off weights $(\alpha, \beta)$ are public political choices rather than smuggled calibration; its failure mode (ranking corpses) is known and guardable by a liveness precondition. Second, *conditional gating* — rank ventures by $\mathrm{PRS}$ only within the population whose institutions clear stated thresholds ($A_7 \geq L$, say) — respects both ledgers without merging them. Third, and most simply, *governance separation*: let the venture ledger allocate project funding and the institutional ledger allocate capacity-building funding, and let no committee demand a number that Theorem 3 shows cannot exist. Impossibility, correctly read, is design guidance.

The result sits in a recognizable lineage, and we state precisely where it does and does not innovate. Arrow (1963) showed that individually reasonable axioms on *preference aggregation across people* are jointly unsatisfiable; Holmström and Milgrom (1991) showed that single-index incentives distort effort across *tasks*; the index-number literature has long known that no single deflator serves all purposes. The boundary lemma's mathematics — annihilation defeats strict separability — is noncompensatory-aggregation territory (Fishburn, 1976; Bouyssou and Marchant, 2007), and the failure of the institutional sector to separate from the venture sector can be phrased in the classical separability vocabulary (Gorman, 1968; Blackorby, Primont and Russell, 1978). What is new, to our knowledge, is the *location* and the *mechanism* of the impossibility: aggregation across observation levels — the project and the institution that hosts it — defeated not by a normative axiom but by two observable facts about how nurseries act on seeds, inside a value model whose channel structure is itself registered for test. The nearest antecedents in evolutionary economics (Nelson and Winter, 1982) argued *informally* that selection environments and organizational routines are incommensurable; Theorem 3 is that intuition made exact, with named premises and testable consequences.

## 5. Operating on the two ledgers: the GO operator

The ledgers are this paper's contribution; we now show they are *operable*. The question every founder–institution pair eventually faces — found now, wait, or stand down — is usually answered by checklist folklore: "the market is hot," "the technology needs one more year," "apply when the program opens." In this section the go/wait decision is *derived* rather than asserted: it emerges as the optimal-stopping rule of a pair that observes the two ledgers and the macro state, and the threshold it stops at is endogenous to project economics. We emphasize the subordination deliberately. The decision operator is not the essence of the model — it is proof that a measurement system built under the discipline of Sections 3–4 leaves nothing operational on the table.

The primitive problem is entry timing under uncertainty (McDonald and Siegel, 1986; Dixit and Pindyck, 1994). A founder–institution pair chooses a stopping time $\tau$ at which to incorporate:
$$\sup_{\tau} \; \mathbb{E}\left[ \int_{\tau}^{\infty} e^{-rt}\big(P \cdot R(t) \cdot S(t) - B(t)\big)\,dt - I \;\Big|\; \mathcal{F}_0 \right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad B(t) - R_{net}(t) \leq F(t),$$
where $B$ is the burn rate, $I$ the irreversible founding cost, $F$ the financial slack available to the pair, $R_{net}$ the venture's self-generated revenue before risk capital, and the constraint states that burn in excess of self-generated revenue must be covered by slack. The macro state $\sigma_{SU}$ follows a three-regime process ($S_0$ dormant, $S_1$ warming, $S_2$ aligned) with transition matrix $\Pi$; the technology gate $g_{TRL}(t) \in \{0,1\}$ ramps deterministically with the maturation program. The information set is exactly the two ledgers plus the macro state — nothing is assumed observable that Section 3 did not construct.

The value function $V(\sigma, F, k)$ — expected value in macro state $\sigma$, slack $F$, regime $k$ — satisfies a system of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations coupled across regimes by $\Pi$. Because regime switching makes the operator non-smooth, existence and uniqueness are established in the viscosity-solution framework (Crandall, Ishii and Lions, 1992); the technical development adds nothing economic and is confined to SM-C. What matters here is that the problem is well-posed: a unique value function exists, and with it a well-defined continuation region in which waiting is strictly optimal.

**Theorem 4** (the GO operator; proof in SM-C). *At the boundary of the continuation region, value-matching and smooth-pasting hold, and their solution defines a unique implicit threshold $\theta_\sigma^*(P, F, B, \Pi)$ per regime such that the optimal founding policy is*
$$\mathrm{GO}(t, i) = \mathbb{1}\big[\sigma_{SU}(t) \geq \theta_\sigma^*\big] \cdot g_{TRL}(t).$$
*The threshold is strictly interior for interior primitives and varies continuously with them.* The folk checklist has become a first-order condition. Two features distinguish this from an off-the-shelf real-options exercise. First, the state being monitored is not a firm-level cash flow — nothing so observable exists before day zero — but the constructed macro state $\sigma_{SU}$ of Section 3, so the stopping rule is only as meaningful as the measurement system beneath it. Second, the threshold is *endogenous*: "how much tailwind is enough" is not a taste parameter but a function of what the project is ($P$), what the pair can afford ($F$, $B$), and how credible the macro regime is ($\Pi$).

**Proposition 2** (comparative statics as policy levers; proof in SM-C). *Within the empirically relevant parameter region: $\partial \theta_\sigma^*/\partial P < 0$; $\partial \theta_\sigma^*/\partial B > 0$; $\partial \theta_\sigma^*/\partial F < 0$; and $\partial \theta_\sigma^*/\partial \pi_{kk} < 0$, with $\theta_\sigma^*(S_0) > \theta_\sigma^*(S_1) > \theta_\sigma^*(S_2)$.* Each derivative is an instrument mapping. A larger addressable market ($P \uparrow$) rationally lowers the tailwind bar — big opportunities justify earlier, riskier entry. Burn pressure ($B \uparrow$) raises it — expensive ventures should demand more alignment before starting the clock. Financial slack ($F \uparrow$) — a gap fund, a fellowship, a patient first customer — lowers it: runway substitutes for tailwind at a rate the model prices. Regime persistence ($\pi_{kk} \uparrow$) lowers it: credible policy commitment is itself a founding subsidy, obtained without spending a yen. Policy instruments that look interchangeable in rhetoric ("support deep-tech startups") move *different arguments of the same threshold*, and Section 7 returns to the design consequences.

The technology gate enters multiplicatively, and the factorization is a theorem rather than an assumption: under conditional independence of the macro regime process and the maturation ramp — an assumption we state, defend, and flag for the registered program — the optimal policy separates as $\mathbb{1}[\sigma \geq \theta_\sigma^*] \cdot g_{TRL}$. The separation carries the section's sharpest practical lesson: *macro-ready but tech-not* and *tech-ready but macro-not* are distinct, non-compensable failure modes. No amount of tailwind repairs an immature technology, and no laboratory perfection conjures an absent market regime. Section 6's first vignette is exactly this square of the matrix, lived by a real project for a decade.

Waiting, in this framework, is not indecision; it is an option held and priced. The continuation value $V - (G - I)$ measures what the pair *owns* by not founding yet — the right to found later on better information — and "too early," the deep-tech graveyard's most common epitaph, acquires a precise meaning: founding while $\sigma_{SU} < \theta_\sigma^*$, i.e., exercising an option whose time value still exceeds its intrinsic value. The field's folk wisdom is thereby quantified rather than dismissed: practitioners who counsel patience are, in the model's terms, correctly pricing an option their committees cannot see.

The institutional ledger, absent from the GO formula, is present throughout its *dynamics* — and only through the C3′ channel that defines Section 4's admissible class. Higher $\mathrm{ERS}$ shifts the drift of $R$ (faster realization under better gap funding and mentoring), improves the hazard structure behind $S$ (survival support), and increases the venture's exposure to favorable $\sigma_{SU}$ innovations (an institution wired into policy and industry translates regime warming into project opportunities faster). Formally, institutional investment shifts $\theta_\sigma^*$'s *arguments* and the state's *law of motion*, so a stronger nursery lowers the effective founding bar — without $\mathrm{ERS}$ ever entering a composite score. This closes the system's logic: the two-ledger governance that Theorem 3 forces is not a constraint we grudgingly accept but a complete design; nothing decision-relevant was lost by refusing the merger.

A scope note maintains the paper's measurement discipline. Everything in this section conditions on the constructed state $\sigma_{SU}$; nothing in this paper estimates its process parameters, identifies regime dates, or claims predictive validation. Those are Tier-B tasks — pre-registered, out-of-sample, multiplicity-corrected — deferred to the registered program of Section 7 and the companion monograph. Here the model is calibrated and exercised; it is not yet tested. Saying so plainly is, we believe, part of what a measurement paper owes its readers.

## 6. Retrospective calibration: eight projects

We now put numbers — deliberately coarse ones — behind the framework. The data are the operating records of a single deep-tech venture studio working with Japanese universities and national laboratories between 2007 and 2026: eight projects whose founding decisions span that period — five with decisions taken and twenty-four-month outcomes complete, one recently founded (outcome right-censored), and two approaching scheduled decision points (readings reported, outcomes pending). Three disclosures govern everything that follows. The cases are *composites*: identifying details are altered and institutions appear as anonymized types, with fidelity maintained on the variables the model reads. The exercise is *retrospective calibration, not validation*: outcomes were known when the rubrics were scored, so agreement demonstrates that the model can organize known history, not that it predicts unknown futures — the registered, prospective test is Section 7's business. And the sample is *selected*: eight projects from one studio's pipeline carry survivorship and idiosyncrasy we do not pretend to correct with $N = 8$; the section's role is illustration under honest labels (hence the Tier-A vocabulary maintained throughout).

Table 2 reports, per project: the $\sigma_{SU}$ regime at the decision point; the technology gate $g_{TRL}$; the slack state $F$; the model's reading ($\mathrm{GO}$ / $\mathrm{WAIT}$ / $\mathrm{NO\_GO}$); the decision actually taken; and a twenty-four-month outcome class (progressing / stagnant / terminated; right-censored where the decision is recent or pending). Scores are reported at rubric level; the calibration constants mapping rubrics to model ranges are described as procedure (Section 3) and withheld as values, consistent with the proprietary-data declaration. Two agreements and one instructive tension organize the reading, and two projects deserve the space of a vignette.

The first vignette is the *zombie type*. Project T (materials; national-university host of type NU-A) sat in a domain whose macro alignment was, by any reading of the momenta, exceptional — construction-sector decarbonization policy, industrial pull, and academic output all warming toward the aligned regime. The technology, however, had never left its originating laboratory: realization readiness at founding was near the rubric floor ($g_{TRL} = 0$ under any defensible gate). The model reads $\mathrm{WAIT}$ — a hold on the technology axis regardless of macro warmth, because the gate multiplies rather than averages. The historical decision was to found early on the strength of the tailwind; the outcome was a decade of stagnation kept alive by the very tailwind that motivated the founding — a zombie, in the field's own argot. The counterfactual is the section's point: *any* monotone composite of a venture score and its glowing macro-institutional environment would have let the environment compensate the missing technology — the Simpson error of Corollary 3.1 committed in a single decision. The two-ledger reading, by refusing the compensation, prices the case correctly ex post.

The second vignette is the *instant-fail type*. Project Y (energy; regional-university host of type RU-B) presents the mirror image: a technology gate that plausibly passes — prototypes existed, the maturation path was legible — attached to a potential-scale factor that unit-economics arithmetic drives to zero at physically attainable performance. Multiplicative annihilation ($P \to 0 \Rightarrow \mathrm{PRS} \to 0$) reads the project as unfoundable at any macro state, which matches the eventual assessment after real resources had been spent discovering it. The instructive comparison is with Project T: an additive or averaged score ranks Y *above* T — decent technology, decent institution, modest macro — precisely inverting the model's (and hindsight's) ordering. The pair of vignettes exhibits the two composite failure modes of Table 1 with live ammunition: addition ranks corpses; unguarded compensation founds zombies.

Across the eight cases read together (Table 2), the type-level pattern is consistent with the causal-channel structure: projects hosted at higher-$\mathrm{ERS}$ types show faster realization drift and better survival trajectories *conditional on* their venture states, while $\mathrm{ERS}$ shows no residual association with outcomes once those trajectories are conditioned on — the qualitative fingerprint C3′ predicts. We state this as a descriptive posterior summary under strong priors and small $N$, not as an identified effect; the distinction is not modesty but method (Section 3's Tier discipline), and the identified version of the claim is precisely what the registered program is built to test.

The limitations paragraph is short because the design already stated most of it: $N = 8$, one studio, one national ecosystem, retrospective scoring, composite disclosure. What the section establishes is deliberately bounded — that the two-ledger system *can* be scored from records that actually exist before day zero, that its readings organize known outcomes without epicycles, and that the composite alternatives fail on these same records in exactly the modes the theory names. What it does not establish is prediction; for that, the model must put its thresholds on the record *before* outcomes arrive, which is the program to which we now turn.

## 7. Policy implications and a research program

For evaluators, the direct instruction is negative and liberating: retire the institution-adjusted single ranking, because Theorem 3 shows the object it approximates does not exist. What replaces it is not chaos but explicit structure. Where a one-dimensional shortlist is genuinely required, use the additive form $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$ *as a decision aid* — with a liveness precondition to guard its known failure mode, and with $(\alpha, \beta)$ published as the political trade-off they are rather than buried as calibration. Where structure permits, prefer conditional gating: rank ventures by the venture ledger within the population whose institutions clear stated thresholds. Both devices are honest because they carry their assumptions on the outside.

For research institutions, the additive form is the point: $\mathrm{ERS}$ is a gap-diagnosis instrument, not a beauty contest. Its decomposability tells a technology-transfer office *which axis*, improved next, moves readiness most per unit of effort — and Proposition 1 orders the sequence: bylaws before showcases, because capabilities behind a missing legal pathway are idle capacity. The temptation the ledger removes is the tournament reading, in which institutions optimize a rank rather than a bottleneck; a published national ranking of $\mathrm{ERS}$ would recreate at the institutional layer exactly the pathology Theorem 3 diagnoses at the merged layer.

For policy design, Proposition 2 converts slogans into comparative statics. Instruments that read as synonyms — "support deep-tech ventures" — act on *different arguments of the founding threshold*: subsidies and demand commitments raise $P$; gap funds and fellowships raise $F$; shared facilities and cost discipline lower $B$; and credible, persistent programs raise $\pi_{kk}$ — the cheapest lever of the four, since regime credibility lowers the founding bar without a yen of transfer. Timing support is likewise a distinct instrument class: extending a viable project's $\mathrm{WAIT}$ (bridging fellowships, laboratory residence) buys option value, where founding pushes spend it — the model prices the difference.

The research program — **Before Zero Studies** — is registered rather than promised. Its falsifiers are stated: the Corollary 3.1 signatures (Simpson reversal, quartile instability, Hausman divergence) under multiplicity-corrected thresholds; channel completeness (C3′) via GMM overidentification on institutional shocks; and, for the operating layer, a prospective registry in which the model's $\mathrm{GO}/\mathrm{WAIT}/\mathrm{NO\_GO}$ readings are filed *before* outcomes arrive, scored on twenty-four-month outcome classes, with a pre-committed discrimination floor (a Brier-score threshold) beneath which the model is declared to have failed (Popper, 1959; Lakatos, 1970). Protocols, rubrics, and outcome definitions are deposited on the Open Science Framework; the registry opens with the studio pipeline described in Section 6 and is built to admit other ecosystems.

The declared boundary bears repeating: deep-tech ventures, Japanese university and national-laboratory origins, the pre-founding stage. We regard portability — to other national systems, to software-dominant ventures, to corporate spin-outs — as the registry's question, not this paper's claim. What we do claim is that within its boundary the framework is complete: measurable with instruments that exist, impossible to collapse into the single score practice keeps demanding, and operable by a rule whose every parameter has an economic name.

## 8. Conclusion

Deep-tech ventures are largely decided before they legally exist, and the deciding stage has lacked a measurement theory. This paper built one: a venture ledger that multiplies because ventures die of their weakest link; an institutional ledger that adds — uniquely, by Theorem 1 — because institutions are portfolios of substitutable capabilities; a macro state that links them; and a proof (Theorem 3) that the two ledgers cannot be merged into any monotone single score once institutional capabilities act stage-contingently through the venture's dynamics. The go/wait decision, so often folklore, emerged as an optimal-stopping rule whose threshold is endogenous to project economics (Theorem 4), and eight retrospectively calibrated projects illustrated both the system's readings and the live failure modes of the composites it replaces.

Before day zero, value is real but unmeasured. The Before Zero Model makes it measurable — and the impossibility theorem stands guard over the practice, ensuring the two ledgers are not quietly re-merged into the convenient number that cannot exist. If the registered program falsifies the framework, the field will have learned something definite; if it survives, evaluation practice acquires foundations where it currently has habits. Either outcome is the founding transaction of Before Zero Studies.
