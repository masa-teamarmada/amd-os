# The Before Zero Model: measuring deep-tech ventures and their institutional nurseries before day zero

**Abstract**: Deep-tech ventures born from universities accumulate — or fail to accumulate — most of their value before incorporation, yet this "Before Zero" stage has no measurement theory. Evaluation practice fills the vacuum with institution-adjusted single scores — a project's rating adjusted by its host institution's capabilities — and committees debate the correct weighting. We prove the debate has no answer. Under four requirements any committee member would separately endorse (a dead venture is worth zero anywhere; a better host never hurts; hosts act on value through what they do to the venture; value moves continuously) together with two facts of technology-transfer practice (institutional capabilities bind at different venture stages, and act over the venture's remaining journey), no monotone single score merging venture and institution can represent venture value — not the current weighting, not any weighting: an impossibility in the Arrow mold. Fields that rank and fund by such scores anyway leave statistical scars, which we derive and pre-register — including a Simpson reversal manufactured by score-based selection itself. What survives is a complete working design: a multiplicative venture ledger ($\mathrm{PRS}$ = potential × realization × survival), an additive institutional ledger ($\mathrm{ERS}$, proven the unique admissible aggregate), a shared Triple-Helix macro state, and a founding-timing rule that reads both ledgers without merging them. Conditional gating — the design the field's most serious agencies already run — is thereby proven right rather than merely customary. A retrospective calibration on eight deep-tech projects (2007–2026) illustrates the system, and a registered falsification program, Before Zero Studies, states in advance the evidence that would prove it wrong.
**Keywords**: deep-tech ventures; university spin-outs; readiness measurement; technology readiness levels; Triple Helix; impossibility theorem; real options; research commercialization

## 1. Introduction

A national screening panel convenes to rank some forty university deep-tech seeds competing for a commercialization fund.¹ Its instrument is an "institution-adjusted project score": each seed's evaluated quality, multiplied by a coefficient reflecting its host university's technology-transfer capabilities. Midway through the session a member proposes re-weighting the institutional coefficient; two seeds swap ranks; a debate follows about which weighting is *correct*. The debate assumes its own answer — that some weighting is correct, that the committee's task is calibration. This paper proves the assumption false. Under requirements every member of that committee would separately endorse — a dead venture is worth zero anywhere; a better institution never hurts — together with two facts of technology-transfer practice — institutional capabilities bind at different venture stages, and act over the venture's remaining journey — *no* institution-adjusted single score exists: not the current weighting, not the proposed one, not any monotone combination however sophisticated. The committee is not miscalibrating an instrument; it is calibrating a nonexistent one.

The setting is the *pre-founding* stage of deep-tech venturing, which we call **Before Zero**: the years — typically five to fifteen — during which a research result inside a university or national laboratory matures toward incorporation ("day zero"). Along that road the decisive assets accumulate: technology readiness, intellectual property, regulatory position, founding team, institutional scaffolding (Shane, 2004; Grimaldi et al., 2011; Perkmann et al., 2013). By day zero, much of a deep-tech venture's fate is already committed. Yet the stage is a measurement vacuum: too early for valuation — no cash flows to discount, no comparables to multiply — it is governed in practice by readiness vocabularies (Mankins, 1995) and scorecards, and increasingly by policy demands for composite rankings of seeds, institutions, and ecosystems (Nardo et al., 2008).

This paper proposes a measurement theory for that vacuum: the **Before Zero Model (BZM)**. Its architecture is deliberately dual — two ledgers with different mathematical shapes. The *venture ledger* scores each project multiplicatively, $\mathrm{PRS} = P \cdot R \cdot S$ (potential scale × realization readiness × survival probability), because a pre-founding venture is a conjunction of necessary conditions in which any zero is fatal. The *institutional ledger* scores the hosting nursery additively, $\mathrm{ERS}$, a weighted sum over eight rubric-scored capability axes, because an institution is a portfolio of partially substitutable capabilities whose gaps must stay visible. A shared macro state $\sigma_{SU}$ — the shifted geometric mean of academic, industrial, and governmental momenta, Triple Helix in conception (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000) — drives the venture ledger's dynamics.

The central result explains why the architecture *must* be dual. We define the institution-adjusted value of a seed honestly — as the expected discounted value of the venture under its institution's nurturing dynamics, the same object the decision layer of Section 5 operates on — so that a dead venture is worth zero anywhere (C1′), a better nursery never hurts (C2′), institutions act on value only through the venture's state dynamics (C3′, the class's defining channel), and value moves continuously (C4′). **Theorem 3** establishes that once institutional capabilities are stage-contingent in their operability — scouting and incubation bind early in the realization journey, licensing and the corporate interface bind late — no single-score representation $\Phi(g(P,R,S), h(A))$ monotone in its indices can represent that value: the true comparison of two nurseries reverses across venture strata, and a single institutional index cannot lie on both sides of a reversal. The composites found in practice die in two distinct ways: pure addition ranks corpses above live ventures at the annihilation boundary, while pure multiplication and every monotone blend — however sophisticated the weighting — fall to the stage-contingency reversal (multiplication, in addition, double-counts a channel that already acts inside the venture's trajectory). **Corollary 3.1** converts the impossibility into empirics: a field that ranks — and therefore funds, and therefore samples — by single scores leaves *statistical scars*: a Simpson reversal of the institution coefficient manufactured by score-selected funding itself, and institutional slopes that shift across decision-time venture strata as stage-contingency requires — registered as diagnostics with tests, conditioning sets, and error rates fixed in advance rather than left as rhetoric.

The result reads plainly. When a committee debates which institutional weighting is *correct*, the debate has no answer: every weighting is right for ventures at one stage of the journey and provably wrong for ventures at another, because what a nursery does for a seed depends on where the seed stands. What the theorem forbids is not evaluation but compression — the two measurements cannot be collapsed into one number that means value. And what survives is everything a working committee actually needs: an additive shortlist whose weights are published as the political choices they are, guarded so that no dead venture outranks a live one; conditional gates — rank ventures on their own merits, fund them only at institutions that clear stated thresholds — which Section 4 proves correct rather than merely customary; and a governance split in which project money follows the venture ledger and capacity-building money follows the institutional ledger. A committee that already gates has been right all along; this paper supplies the reason it is right, and Section 7 supplies the audit answer for the ministry that demands the composite anyway.

Two further results complete the system. **Theorem 1** shows the weighted sum is not a convenient choice but the *unique* institutional aggregate — in rubric units, given the rubric's equal-interval reporting convention — admissible under five practice-grounded axioms (separability, compensability, audited missing-visibility, monotonicity, cross-context invariance), by additive conjoint measurement (Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky, 1971); a precondition gate for formal bylaws (Proposition 1) sharpens it in the spirit of Alkire and Foster (2011). **Theorem 4** then shows the system is operable: the go/wait decision is formalized as an optimal-stopping rule in the regime-switching real-options tradition (Dixit and Pindyck, 1994; Guo, Miao and Morellec, 2005), with a threshold curve $\theta_\sigma^*(k;F)$ derived from project economics ($P$, burn, runway, founding cost, regime credibility) rather than asserted as folklore — and, distinctively, monitored on a state variable *constructed by the auditable measurement layer of Section 3* — together with comparative statics that map policy instruments to distinct channels of the threshold. We order the contributions deliberately: measurement first; the decision operator demonstrates the measurement's completeness rather than headlining the paper.

We exercise the system on the operating records of a single venture studio: eight deep-tech projects across Japanese universities and national laboratories, 2007–2026, reported as composites with type-named institutions. The exercise is retrospective calibration under explicit Tier discipline — outcomes were known when rubrics were scored, so it demonstrates organization of history, not prediction. Its two vignettes are nonetheless instructive: a macro-blessed, technology-hollow project that any composite would have founded (and history did found, into a decade of stagnation), and a technically presentable project whose potential-scale zero the multiplicative ledger catches while additive scores rank it above the first. The genuinely predictive test — prospective, pre-registered, multiplicity-corrected — is specified as a research program rather than claimed as an accomplishment.

Three contributions, then, to this journal's readership. *A measurement theory for an unmeasured stage*: axiomatic foundations for evaluating ventures that do not yet exist, where valuation is structurally silent. *An impossibility as policy guardrail*: proof that the single-score instrument evaluation practice keeps demanding cannot exist, together with what legitimately survives — additive shortlists with public weights, conditional gates, and governance separation of the two ledgers. *A falsification-registered research program* — **Before Zero Studies** — whose central claims are built to be testable and whose violation signatures are specified in advance (Popper, 1959).

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 locates the gap at the intersection of five literatures. Section 3 constructs the two ledgers and proves the institutional aggregate unique. Section 4 states the axioms and the impossibility theorem, with its falsifiable corollary. Section 5 derives the GO operator and its comparative statics. Section 6 reports the retrospective calibration. Section 7 draws design implications and registers the program; Section 8 concludes. Scope is declared at the outset: deep-tech ventures, Japanese university and national-laboratory context, pre-founding stage. Whether the theory travels is an empirical question the program is built to answer, not a claim we make.

## 2. Two evaluation problems, one field

Readiness scales are the pre-founding evaluator's incumbent technology. Technology Readiness Levels, born as a NASA communication protocol for hardware maturity (Mankins, 1995), have diffused far beyond their origin: European framework programs mandate them, national funding agencies gate on them, and the family has multiplied into business, societal, and organizational variants — the European Commission's Societal Readiness Level among them. Their strength is real: a shared ordinal ladder disciplines conversation across communities that otherwise talk past each other. Their limits are equally real and well documented (Olechowski et al., 2020): each scale is a single ordinal axis with no aggregation theory — nothing in the TRL apparatus says how technology maturity should combine with market, team, or institutional maturity into an assessment of the *venture*, and nothing prevents practitioners from averaging ordinal levels as if they were cardinal quantities. Readiness scales, in short, supply vocabulary without measurement theory.

Where multiple axes must become one number, evaluation practice reaches for composite indicators, and here the methodological literature has long sounded alarms. The canonical handbook (Nardo et al., 2008) catalogs the arbitrariness of weighting and normalization choices; systematic reviews document rank reversals under defensible alternative constructions (Greco et al., 2019); and the deepest critique is Sen's (1977): an index that buries heterogeneous dimensions in a single number conceals precisely the trade-offs that deliberation should surface. The constructive exception proves instructive — the Alkire–Foster family of multidimensional poverty measures (Alkire and Foster, 2011) survives scrutiny because it separates *identification* (who is poor) from *aggregation* (how poor overall), axiomatizing each step. Innovation evaluation has imported the composite habit without importing the axiomatic discipline; this paper's Proposition 1 and Theorem 3 are, in one reading, that discipline arriving.

Nor is the demand hypothetical: the merger is load-bearing inside the world's largest evaluation systems. The U.S. National Institutes of Health long scored five review criteria — among them the host *environment* — and merged them into the single Overall Impact score that orders its funding; its simplified review framework, in force since January 2025, removed the numerical score from precisely the expertise-and-resources factor, converting it to a sufficiency judgment — a system-scale move away from merged scoring and toward the gate structure this paper derives [verify at S6: NIH NOT-OD-24-010]. The United Kingdom currently operates both sides of the question: the Research Excellence Framework merges an institutional *environment* profile, at a stated weight, with outputs and impact into the single quality profile that allocates public research funding, while its knowledge-exchange counterpart, the KEF, deliberately publishes perspective dashboards and refuses any composite ranking [verify at S6]. National innovation scoreboards compress whole systems into single indices whose fragility is documented in this journal (Grupp and Mogee, 2004; Grupp and Schubert, 2010). The merger this paper studies is not a straw man; it is the modal instrument — and where practice has begun abandoning it, it has done so by instinct, without the theorem that says the instinct is sound.

A third literature studies our institutional layer directly. Technology-transfer offices, gap funds, incubators, entrepreneurs-in-residence, and spin-out policies have each generated substantial research programs (Bozeman, 2000; Shane, 2004; Grimaldi et al., 2011; Munari et al., 2016; Hayter et al., 2018), and their collective finding is that institutional capabilities materially shape spin-out formation and survival. The stage those capabilities serve is the institutional face of the funding and attention gap known as the valley of death (Auerswald and Branscomb, 2003), and the stage-wise view of that crossing — spin-outs developing through critical junctures at which different resources and capabilities bind (Vohora, Wright and Lockett, 2004) — is precisely the stage-contingency that Section 4 turns into an impossibility. Yet this literature measures the institution *separately* from the venture: its dependent variables are counts, rates, and survival curves at the institution or policy level. Evaluation practice, meanwhile, merges the two levels daily — every "university-adjusted" project ranking and every "ecosystem-weighted" seed score is such a merger — and the merger itself has, to our knowledge, never been theorized. Deployed instruments do exist at the institutional layer — HEInnovate's self-assessment dimensions, the Knowledge Exchange Framework's perspective dashboards, the AUTM and ASTP benchmarking surveys [verify at S6] — and Section 7 positions our ledger against each of them; none, however, carries an aggregation theory, and none theorizes the cross-level merger. The gap this paper fills is not the study of institutions or of ventures but of the *aggregation across levels* that practice performs without a license.

The Triple Helix program (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000) supplies our macro layer's conceptual frame: innovation capacity emerges from the co-evolution of academia, industry, and government, and one strand has made the frame quantitative through mutual-information synergy indicators (Leydesdorff, 2003). What the program lacks is a *venture-facing* state variable — a quantity a founder–institution pair can observe and a stopping rule can condition on. The synergy indicator $T_{AIG}$ diagnoses a national or regional *system*; no decision about a particular seed follows from it. Our $\sigma_{SU}$ is built to occupy exactly this vacant slot — Triple Helix in conception, decision-theoretic in function — and Section 3 is careful to keep the two objects distinct rather than claiming to improve one with the other.

Entry timing under uncertainty is the fifth ingredient, and the real-options literature supplies it in mature form: irreversible investment, option value of waiting, threshold policies (McDonald and Siegel, 1986; Dixit and Pindyck, 1994), with applications to R&D and staged innovation investment (McGrath, 1997). The specific apparatus Section 5 uses is likewise mature: entry thresholds per regime on Markov-modulated dynamics, with comparative statics in the switching generator (Guo and Zhang, 2004; Guo, Miao and Morellec, 2005; Jobert and Rogers, 2006), policy uncertainty as a timing determinant (Hassett and Metcalf, 1999), investment timing under a liquidity constraint with a cash-stock state (Boyle and Guthrie, 2003), and stopping under incomplete information about the environment (Décamps, Mariotti and Villeneuve, 2005). One feature of all of it fails before day zero: the monitored state is a firm-level cash flow or output price — observable for going concerns, nonexistent for pre-founding ventures — and the threshold, however elegantly derived, floats free of any *measurement system*: the literature says "invest when the state crosses the bar" without saying how the state is to be constructed for an entity that does not yet exist. Section 5 marries that stopping machinery to the measurement layer of Section 3, which is, we believe, the first such marriage in this domain; the marriage, not the machinery, is the claim.

Finally, the tools of this paper's central result come from a tradition innovation studies has rarely visited: the measurement-theoretic and social-choice lineage. Arrow (1963) established the template — reasonable axioms, jointly unsatisfiable — for preference aggregation; Debreu (1960) and the conjoint-measurement school (Luce and Tukey, 1964; Krantz et al., 1971; Wakker, 1989) characterized exactly when multi-attribute orderings admit additive or separable representations, with sign-consistency and the Thomsen/double-cancellation condition as the load-bearing tests. These results are sixty years old, rigorous, and almost entirely absent from the evaluation practices they indict. Importing them is this paper's methodological move: Theorem 1 applies Krantz–Luce–Suppes–Tversky machinery to an institutional object those authors never considered, and the impossibility of Section 4 rebuilds Arrow's template — individually reasonable requirements, a richness condition on the admissible domain, joint unsatisfiability — at the venture–institution interface, where the richness is supplied not by preference profiles but by how nurseries act on seeds.

The synthesis, then: five literatures each hold one piece — vocabulary (readiness scales), aggregation warnings (composite indicators), the institutional layer (technology transfer), the macro frame (Triple Helix), and timing (real options) — and none holds the piece that connects them: a measurement theory for the pre-founding venture that respects venture multiplicativity, derives institutional additivity, proves the two layers cannot be merged, and hands the result to a stopping rule. The Before Zero Model is our attempt to build that connective theory, and everything in it is constructed so that its central claims can be wrong in public: the axioms are stated for inspection, and the impossibility's violation signatures are registered for detection.

## 3. The two-layer observation system

Consider a research seed $i$ hosted by an institution $j$ — a university, a national laboratory, or a public research organization — at time $t$ before incorporation. What can actually be observed? Not cash flows, market shares, or churn: none of these exist yet. What can be observed are rubric-scored states: the maturity of the technology and of the surrounding preparations, the institution's own capabilities (its bylaws, licensing practice, gap funding, entrepreneurial staffing), and the macro environment in which academia, industry, and government pay attention to the seed's domain. The Before Zero measurement problem is to organize these observables so that they support decisions — without pretending to a precision that the stage cannot deliver. Our answer is deliberately dual: two ledgers with different mathematical shapes, plus one macro state variable they share.

The **venture ledger** assigns to each project the triple $(P, R, S)$ and the score
$$\mathrm{PRS}_{i}(t) = P_i(t) \cdot R_i(t) \cdot S_i(t),$$
where $P$ is the *potential scale* of the opportunity conditional on full realization, $R$ is *realization readiness* — how far the technology, team, and commercialization preparations have actually progressed toward that potential — and $S$ is the *non-financing survival probability* that the venture-to-be endures long enough to complete the journey — technical, team, and market mortality; funding exhaustion is priced separately and dynamically as the runway barrier of Section 5, so no death channel is counted twice. The multiplicative form is not a stylistic choice. A pre-founding venture is a conjunction of necessary conditions, and production processes whose success requires every link to hold are weakest-link economies in the sense of the O-ring literature (Kremer, 1993): excellence in two factors cannot rescue a zero in the third. A seed with world-class science but a market of zero economic size, or a vast market but a technology that cannot leave the laboratory, is not "average"; it is dead. Multiplication, unlike any averaging rule, encodes this mortality.

How $(P, R, S)$ are scored is part of the model's content, not an implementation detail, and we fix it before anything is aggregated. Each factor is scored on a **five-level ordinal rubric anchored by observable states** — evidence documents and demonstrated capabilities, not judgment scales — and every reading is taken *at the decision date, from the records available at that date*: for $R$, the documented maturation state of technology, team, and commercialization preparations (a prototype demonstrated outside its originating laboratory, a key hire signed, a regulatory pre-consultation held); for $S$, the evidenced mortality structure (single-point technical dependencies, team fragility, the documented state of the market window); for $P$, the diligence objects defined next. The $P$ construct requires the most care, because practice runs two different diligence objects together under "market size." $P$ is **attainable-unit-economics-priced addressable scale**: the value ceiling of the opportunity conditional on full realization, priced at physically attainable performance and cost. Under this definition a market-size zero (no demand at any price) and a margin-viability zero (unit economics infeasible at attainable performance, so each unit sold destroys value) are both $P$-zeros — the ceiling is zero either way, and the ledger does not care which arithmetic delivered it. Project Y's $P \to 0$ in Section 6 is a viability zero, not a size zero; the rubric anchors keep the two sub-objects visible so that a healthy reading on one cannot be quoted for the other.

Three scoring disciplines complete the operationalization. *Information sets are dated*: retrospective scoring (Section 6) admits only evidence knowable at the decision date, and the registry's prospective readings are timestamped before outcomes arrive (Section 7), so the information set is auditable in both directions. *Measurement capacity is endogenous*: scoring $P$ at rubric discipline requires unit-economics diligence — capacity of exactly the kind axis $A_1$ below measures — so the quality of a venture-ledger reading depends on who reads it, an argument for scoring at the agency or studio level rather than by self-assessment. *Domain boundaries are fixed ex ante*: the seed's domain — the object over which the macro momenta of the next paragraph are computed — is set in the deposited protocol before scoring and is contestable on the record, because the domain is the most gameable object in the system (whoever draws the boundary chooses the tailwind), and the protocol treats it as such. Finally, the disclosure architecture is a declared layer split: the theorems, the rubrics, and the anchor logic are public, while the studio's fitted calibration constants — the mapping of rubric levels into the model's numerical ranges — are proprietary. The split is stated as a split: everything an adopting evaluator needs in order to rescore from its own records is on the public side of it.

The two ledgers share one **macro state**. Let $\mu_A$, $\mu_I$, and $\mu_G$ denote the momenta of academic, industrial, and governmental attention to the seed's domain — publication dynamics, industrial commitment, and policy budgets, each observed as a time series. Define
$$\sigma_{SU}(t) = \sqrt[3]{(\mu_A + 1)(\mu_I + 1)(\mu_G + 1)} - 1,$$
the shifted geometric mean of the three momenta. Conceptually this is a Triple Helix object (Etzkowitz and Leydesdorff, 2000): the claim embedded in the geometric-mean form — an equal-weight Cobb–Douglas aggregate of the shifted momenta (Cobb and Douglas, 1928) — is that macro tailwind is a *joint* property of the three helices — government money without industrial pull, or academic fashion without policy commitment, does not constitute alignment. We stress that $\sigma_{SU}$ is not Leydesdorff's (2003) mutual-information indicator $T_{AIG}$: the two measure different things (a venture-facing state variable versus a system-level synergy diagnostic), respond to different perturbations, and serve different decision problems; a formal cross-walk is developed in the companion monograph. In what follows, $\sigma_{SU}$ evolves through discrete regimes (dormant $S_0$, warming $S_1$, aligned $S_2$), and the venture ledger's dynamics — in particular the drift of $R$ and the hazard structure behind $S$ — condition on it.

The **institutional ledger** observes the nursery rather than the seed. Institution $j$ is scored on eight axes $A_1, \ldots, A_8$ (each on a 0–4 rubric; the full rubric is summarized in Supplementary Material D): (1) seed scouting and technology assessment; (2) IP management and licensing (TLO function); (3) incubation and entrepreneurship support; (4) industry-collaboration and corporate interface; (5) funding access, including gap funds; (6) entrepreneurial management talent (EIR/CXO supply); (7) entrepreneurship bylaws and governance; and (8) government and policy linkage. Five axioms discipline how these observations may be aggregated. **E1 (separability)**: the contribution of one axis to institutional readiness can be assessed without knowing the level of every other axis. **E2 (compensability)**: institutional capabilities partially substitute — a thin incubation program can be compensated, at some rate, by a strong industry interface or external fund access; no axis *other than the bylaws precondition* (axis 7, whose special status Proposition 1 formalizes) is individually fatal to the *institution* (unlike the venture's factors, where any zero kills). **E3 (audited missing-visibility)**: an unobserved axis must remain visible as missing rather than silently imputed — "unknown" and "not started" are different states (Rubin, 1976) — and the observed set is fixed by the assessor's evidence standard: an institution that *declines to evidence* an axis is scored at the rubric floor rather than treated as unobserved, so opacity cannot raise a score (SM-A develops the anti-gaming protocol). **E4 (monotonicity)**: improving any observed axis raises institutional readiness — no axis is inert. **E5 (cross-context invariance)**: the rate at which two observed capabilities trade against each other does not depend on which further axes happen to be observed — an assessor who has calibrated how gap-fund access trades against incubation capacity does not recalibrate that judgment because the bylaws axis was unobservable at one institution. Each axiom is a statement about technology-transfer practice before it is mathematics: committees do assess bylaws without auditing the incubator, networks do substitute for in-house capacity, and honest gap diagnosis requires that ignorance stay visible without rewarding it.

**Theorem 1** (additive representation; proof in SM-A). *Let institutional readiness be a family of continuous orderings, one for each observed set $K_{obs} \subseteq \{1,\ldots,8\}$, satisfying E1–E5 together with the standard conjoint-measurement solvability and Archimedean conditions and the Thomsen/double-cancellation property (on the continuum idealization of the rubric stated in SM-A), and let the rubric carry the equal-interval anchor convention (E2-s). Then a single weight vector $w_1, \ldots, w_8 > 0$ serves every observed set, and each ordering is representable as*
$$\mathrm{ERS}_j = 100 \cdot \frac{\sum_{k \in K_{obs}} w_k A_{jk}}{4 \sum_{k \in K_{obs}} w_k},$$
*unique up to positive affine transformation among scores additive in rubric units.* The result is an application of additive conjoint measurement (Krantz, Luce, Suppes and Tversky, 1971, ch. 6; Debreu, 1960; Wakker, 1989), with the cross-context clause a cousin of subgroup consistency in poverty measurement (Foster and Shorrocks, 1991), and its content deserves careful statement: the weighted sum in rubric units is the *unique aggregate consistent with an equal-interval reporting convention* — without E2-s the same axioms deliver a weighted sum of monotone recalibrations of each axis, so what the convention buys is exactly the linearity committees audit. The ratios $w_k/w_{k'}$ are empirical content recovered from observed trade-offs; the paper's default of equal weights is a disclosed working convention pending that elicitation, not a consequence of the axioms. Whereas the venture ledger multiplies because ventures die of their weakest link, the institutional ledger adds because institutions are portfolios of partially substitutable capabilities.

One qualification sharpens the additive form rather than contradicting it. **Proposition 1** (precondition gate). *Axis 7 — entrepreneurship bylaws and formal rules — enters not as an additive term but as a gate: $\mathrm{ERS}_j = 100 \cdot g(A_{j7}) \cdot F(A_{j,-7})$, where $g$ is a step (or steep sigmoid) function and $F$ is the Theorem-1 aggregate over the remaining axes.* An institution scoring 44% on capabilities but with no legal pathway for a professor to found a company has idle capacity, not readiness: every other capability waits on a rule that does not exist. The structure mirrors the identification-then-aggregation discipline of multidimensional poverty measurement (Alkire and Foster, 2011) — first determine whether the institution is *in the game*, then measure how much of the game it has built. Formally, axioms E1–E5 govern the seven non-gate axes, and axis 7 enters through the gate premise alone — E2 is withdrawn for it by design — so the gate refines Theorem 1 rather than contradicting it (the gate's own axioms, and the consistency of the block, are made explicit in SM-A).

Before asking whether the two ledgers can be merged into one number, note what is already prohibited. **Theorem 2** (no multiplicative cross-combination; proof in SM-A). *Any multiplicative cross-combination $\mathrm{ERS}^{\alpha} \cdot \mathrm{PRS}^{1-\alpha}$, $\alpha \in (0,1)$, preserves the institutional ordering at each fixed venture state — but destroys the calibrated reporting scale of the institutional ledger: rubric steps no longer trade at level-independent rates within the institutional layer, and the marginal credit for every institutional axis becomes contingent on the venture state, so the nursery is paid once inside the venture score and again as a multiplier.* The claim is deliberately cardinal: what the merger breaks is the interval-scale report by which committees audit published weights — the property the equal-interval convention delivers in Theorem 1 — and the venture-contingent credit is double counting, whose full value-level form Section 4 proves. Theorem 2 thus disqualifies the most popular composite as a *reporting instrument*; Section 4 asks the general question: is *any* single score possible?

The economic logic behind the shape difference bears stating plainly, because it drives everything that follows. A venture is a *conjunction*: every necessary condition must hold, so the informative question is "what kills this deal?" — and the multiplicative ledger makes the killer visible as the smallest factor. An institution is a *portfolio*: capabilities substitute at the margin, so the informative question is "what should we build next?" — and the additive ledger makes the answer visible as the largest weighted gap. Forcing either object into the other's shape destroys precisely the information the evaluator needs: an additive venture score hides the fatal zero; a multiplicative institution score turns one weak axis into a false death sentence.

Operationalization follows measurement-theoretic discipline. All axis scores are ordinal rubrics (Stevens, 1946) anchored by observable states, not judgment scales; the distinction between "unknown" and "not started" is coded explicitly and propagates to $K_{obs}$ (Little and Rubin, 2019); institutions are reported as anonymized types and projects as composites (Section 6); and the calibration constants that map rubric levels into the model's numerical ranges are treated as a *calibration procedure* — reported as method, not as results — both to respect proprietary operating data and because the theorems of this paper are invariant to them.

## 4. The impossibility theorem

The temptation this section formalizes is ubiquitous. An evaluation committee holds a venture-level score and an institution-level score and wants one number per (project, institution) pair: $f(P, R, S; A) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, an "institution-adjusted project value" by which to rank a national portfolio of seeds, allocate a gap fund, or compare universities' pipelines. The demand is reasonable on its face — decisions are one-dimensional, so why not the measure? We take the demand seriously and make its target precise — and prove the target empty: once two observable facts about how nurseries act on seeds are admitted, no monotone merger of the two ledgers can represent venture value.

The first step is to say what an "institution-adjusted project value" honestly *is*. A live seed's value is not a static attribute of its current readings; it is the expected discounted value of what the venture will become under its institution's care — the same object Section 5 operates on. We therefore define $f(P, R, S; A)$ as the value function of a *nurturing environment*: the venture state evolves under laws of motion whose coefficients — the drift of $R$, the hazard behind $S$, the slack $F$, the exposure to $\sigma_{SU}$ innovations — depend on the institutional profile $A$, and $f$ is the expected discounted payoff under those dynamics (the admissible class, and a deterministic benchmark subclass in which every proof below is elementary calculus, are specified in SM-B). On this definition, three requirements that would otherwise have to be postulated become structural properties. **C1′ (venture annihilation)**: a venture with no market or no survival — $\min(P, S) = 0$ — is absorbed at zero value regardless of $A$: nobody funds a dead project because its university has excellent bylaws. Realization is deliberately *excluded* from the annihilation set: $R = 0$ is the normal state of an early seed, and its value is an option, not a corpse — gap funds exist precisely to fund $R \approx 0$ projects on institution and potential. (A founder-side microfoundation of C1′ is treated in the companion monograph; the property stands on the mortality argument alone.) **C2′ (institutional monotonicity)**: a better nursery never makes a live project less valuable — improving any axis improves drifts and hazards, hence value. **C4′ (continuity)**: value moves continuously in states and profiles. The fourth item is not a derived property but the class's defining restriction. **C3′ (causal channel)**: institutional axes enter *only* the laws of motion of the venture's state — realization, survival, slack, exposure — and carry no direct premium at a fixed venture path. This is a causal statement with empirical content: bylaws, gap funds, and mentors matter because they accelerate realization, improve survival, and buy runway — not because a committee enjoys the institution's company. A brand or halo channel, by which measured value rises in $A$ with the venture's trajectory held fixed, is excluded by the class definition — and is exactly what the generalized-method-of-moments overidentification test registered in Section 7 is built to detect (Hansen, 1982), so the restriction is submitted to data rather than asserted as dogma.

The channel restriction must face, rather than wave at, the strongest empirical objection to it. A substantial literature documents *certification effects*: affiliation with prominent partners raises young ventures' survival and speeds their capital acquisition (Stuart, Hoang and Hybels, 1999); entrepreneurs accept discounted terms to obtain high-reputation venture backing (Hsu, 2004); early-stage public grants causally improve subsequent private financing (Howell, 2017) — and every practitioner in our context knows the folk version, the premium a national-university seal commands. Do these findings break C3′? The class answers with a distinction between *price* and *value*. What certification demonstrably moves is the venture's access to resources: capital arrives sooner, on better terms, from better partners. In the model those are trajectory consequences — endorsement and access operate on the slack $F$, the survival hazard, and the exposure to $\sigma_{SU}$ innovations, carriers *inside* the licensed channel, and SM-D routes exactly the axes that carry certification in practice ($A_4$, $A_5$, $A_8$) through them. What the class excludes is only the residual: a premium on measured value at a *fixed* venture trajectory — a halo that changes what a committee writes down without changing what the venture does or survives. That residual is precisely what the Section 7 overidentification test estimates; certification effects that work through trajectories sit inside its null, not against it. And the theorem degrades gracefully if the test rejects: Theorem 3's proof uses only the reversal condition SH, a directly observable property of value orderings, so a documented direct channel would change the class's interpretation — value partly constituted by perception — without rescuing the single score, unless the direct premium were strong and uniform enough to undo every stage-contingent reversal: itself an empirical question, and one the registered program is built to price.

The theorem needs two further ingredients, and they are facts about nurseries rather than mathematical conveniences. **SH (stage-contingent operability)**: different institutional capabilities bind at different stages of the realization journey. Scouting and incubation act on a seed still finding its shape; licensing execution and the corporate interface act on a seed approaching the market — the critical-junctures sequence long documented for university spin-outs (Vohora, Wright and Lockett, 2004). Consequently the value ranking of two institutional profiles can genuinely *reverse* across venture strata: for an early seed, the incubation-strong nursery is worth more than the licensing-strong one; for a late seed, the reverse (SM-B.3 exhibits the reversal in closed form). **ED (exposure-duration asymmetry)**: whatever an institution does, it does over the venture's *remaining* journey, so institutional strength compounds strongly for an early seed with a long road left and weakly for a seed nearly done. Consequently the ranking of two *ventures* can reverse as their shared institution strengthens (SM-B.3 again). Both conditions are observable, both are standard talk in technology-transfer practice, and both enter the registered program as premises to be tested, not as dogma.

One preliminary result disposes of a class practice does not actually use. Any composite $\Phi(g(P,R,S), h(A))$ with $\Phi$ *strictly* increasing in both arguments is inconsistent with annihilation alone: on the death set $\min(P,S)=0$ every institutional comparison ties at zero, while strict monotonicity in the institutional index forces strict gaps (Lemma SM-B.2). We state this as a lemma, not a theorem, because it is easy — an instance, transported across observation levels, of the familiar fact that veto and annihilation structures defeat strictly monotone separable representations (Fishburn, 1976; Bouyssou and Marchant, 2007) — and because it has no bite against practice: the composites committees actually field are *weakly* monotone, flat in the institution exactly where the venture is dead. Pure multiplication, geometric and CES blends, and min-gates all pass the boundary test. The question that matters is whether any weakly monotone merger survives the two facts of the preceding paragraph.

**Theorem 3** (single-index impossibility; proof in SM-B). *Let venture value be generated by any admissible nurturing environment satisfying SH (respectively ED). Then no functions $g$, $h$ and no merging rule $\Phi$ non-decreasing in its institutional argument (respectively its venture argument) represent the value ordering: $f = \Phi\big(g(P,R,S),\, h(A)\big)$ fails on a pair of live, interior states.* The argument is short enough to state. Under SH there are two live venture states and two institutional profiles whose value comparison reverses across the states. A single score reads the institution through one index $h(A)$: at the early state the representation forces $h(A) > h(A')$, and monotonicity of $\Phi$ then forces the composite to rank $A$ above $A'$ at *every* venture state — including the late state, where the true ranking has reversed. No calibration escapes: nothing is assumed about $g$ and $h$ beyond their existence, so the exclusion covers every weighting a committee might tune — multiplication, weighted addition, geometric means, CES mixes, min-gated and lexicographic hybrids, and every monotone recalibration of any of them. Under ED the same dichotomy runs through the venture index. A composite that is *not* monotone in its own indices defeats the purpose of scoring — no committee fields an instrument on which a better institution score lowers the composite — so the demanded object does not exist. The committee of Section 1 is not miscalibrating an instrument; it is calibrating a nonexistent one.

Two remarks locate the content. First, the dynamics are not decoration. On a static domain, weakly monotone single scores consistent with annihilation and monotonicity *do* exist — $f = \min(\mathrm{PRS}, \kappa(A))$ with $\kappa$ a calibrated institutional index is one — and no axiom system that constrains a static $f$ pointwise can exclude them. What excludes them is stage-contingency of the dynamics: under SH the true institutional comparison lies on both sides of a reversal that any single index must call one way (SM-B.4). The dynamic foundation is the source of the theorem's content, not a technical reformulation. Second, the impossibility bites where committees operate: its witnesses are interior, live states — pre-screened portfolios — not boundary pathologies; the death boundary is needed only for the easy lemma.

The portfolio version supplies the Arrow-style reading. **Corollary 3.2** (portfolio impossibility; SM-B.5). *If the portfolio spans technology domains rich enough that some institutional axis is operative for one seed's environment and inert for another's, then no environment-blind score $F(P,R,S;A)$ — separable or not — represents value across the portfolio.* The proof is immediate, and we flag as much; the content is in the domain condition, exactly as in preference aggregation, where it is the richness of admissible profiles, not any single profile, that defeats the rule (Arrow, 1963). One formula, forty seeds, forty response structures: the formula must misrank somewhere, and Corollary 3.1 says the misranking is statistically visible.

Table 1 organizes the disposal of the four families found in practice — three that merge the ledgers and fail, and a fourth that survives by not merging them. *Pure addition* $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$ dies at the boundary: a dead venture at a strong institution outranks a live venture at a weak one — the ranking rewards the nursery for the corpse (and, being monotone, it falls to Theorem 3 besides). *Pure multiplication* $\mathrm{PRS} \cdot \mathrm{ERS}$ passes the boundary — a zero kills the product — but is a monotone composite, so Theorem 3 applies; it moreover double-counts the institution, whose capabilities already act inside $R$, $S$, and $F$ by C3′, and Theorem 2 excludes it at the ledger level. *General monotone blends* — geometric, CES, min-gated, lexicographic — are Theorem 3's direct quarry. The fourth family is different in kind. *Gated-additive designs* — additive scoring subject to per-criterion thresholds, the standard evaluation model of Horizon Europe [verify at S6], and institutional certification gates on venture-ranked populations in national programs [verify at S6] — never construct the object Theorem 3 forbids: where the gate is institutional and the ranking is venture-level, the two ledgers are consulted without being merged into one score. The three merging families each fail for their own reason, which is why practitioners patching one failure reliably create another; the fourth survives — it is Section 7's conditional gating, already fielded at scale, and the theorems of this section are, among other things, an explanation of why the agencies that run it are right.

**Corollary 3.1** (registered diagnostics; SM-B.6). *Let outcomes be generated by an admissible environment with operative, stage-contingent channels, and let venture strata be decision-time $\mathrm{PRS}$-quartiles — timestamped before the institution acts on the remaining journey. Then: (ii) quartile instability — within-stratum institutional slopes positive at operative strata, collapsing at the annihilated stratum, and unequal across strata, varying with each axis's operative stage under SH and scaling with remaining exposure under ED — holds as a derived proposition (SM-B.6, Proposition SM-B.2); (iii), its test form, is the cluster-robust Wald (slope-equality) test on the $\mathrm{ERS}\times\mathrm{PRS}$-stratum interactions; and (i) Simpson reversal — a pooled institution coefficient opposite in sign to the within-stratum coefficients (Simpson, 1951) — is derived under one further, auditable premise: the field funds by a single monotone score, so the observed portfolio is selected on that score, and score selection is collider conditioning that induces negative venture–institution dependence in the funded sample, with a closed-form reversal condition (SM-B.6, Lemma SM-B.3 and Proposition SM-B.1).* The corollary's logic is thereby sharper than "misspecification leaves traces": signature (i) exists *because* the field ranks by one score — the ranking practice manufactures the scar it leaves, and under the conditional gating this paper recommends the scar vanishes identically — while signature (ii) survives score-selected sampling and tests the stage-contingency itself. These are registered diagnostics under a single family-wise error rate, with estimators, conditioning sets, and power requirements fixed in advance (SM-B.6; Section 7) — not a deterministic claim that any particular dataset must exhibit them. (The Hausman (1978) estimator contrast survives in SM-B.6 as a remark, with corrected degrees of freedom and its efficiency caveat; the Wald form is the registered primary.)

What survives the impossibility is precisely what committees should build instead — and, in its gated form, what the field's most serious agencies already run. First, *additive shortlisting* $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$ remains available — not as a measurement, which C1′ forbids it to be, but as an explicit decision aid whose trade-off weights $(\alpha, \beta)$ are public political choices rather than smuggled calibration; its failure mode (ranking corpses) is known and guardable by a liveness precondition. Second, *conditional gating* — rank ventures by $\mathrm{PRS}$ only within the population whose institutions clear stated thresholds ($A_7 \geq L$, say) — respects both ledgers without merging them; it is Table 1's fourth family in its clean form, and Section 7 locates its deployed instances and what they still get wrong. Third, and most simply, *governance separation*: let the venture ledger allocate project funding and the institutional ledger allocate capacity-building funding, and let no committee demand a number that Theorem 3 shows cannot exist. Impossibility, correctly read, is design guidance.

The result sits in a recognizable lineage, and we state precisely where it does and does not innovate. Arrow (1963) showed that individually reasonable axioms on *preference aggregation across people* are jointly unsatisfiable; Holmström and Milgrom (1991) showed that single-index incentives distort effort across *tasks*; the index-number literature has long known that no single deflator serves all purposes. The boundary lemma's mathematics — annihilation defeats strict separability — is noncompensatory-aggregation territory (Fishburn, 1976; Bouyssou and Marchant, 2007), and the failure of the institutional sector to separate from the venture sector can be phrased in the classical separability vocabulary (Gorman, 1968; Blackorby, Primont and Russell, 1978). What is new, to our knowledge, is the *location* and the *mechanism* of the impossibility: aggregation across observation levels — the project and the institution that hosts it — defeated not by a normative axiom but by two observable facts about how nurseries act on seeds, inside a value model whose channel structure is itself registered for test. The nearest antecedents in evolutionary economics (Nelson and Winter, 1982) argued *informally* that selection environments and organizational routines are incommensurable; Theorem 3 is that intuition made exact, with named premises and testable consequences.

## 5. Operating on the two ledgers: the GO operator

The ledgers are this paper's contribution; we now show they are *operable*. The question every founder–institution pair eventually faces — found now, wait, or stand down — is usually answered by checklist folklore: "the market is hot," "the technology needs one more year," "apply when the program opens." In this section the go/wait decision is *derived* rather than asserted: it emerges as the optimal-stopping rule of a pair that observes the constructed macro state, its own funds stock, and the venture ledger's response structure, and the threshold it stops at is a function of project economics rather than taste. We emphasize the subordination deliberately. The decision operator is not the essence of the model — it is proof that a measurement system built under the discipline of Sections 3–4 leaves nothing operational on the table. And we position it honestly: the stopping mathematics is inherited, not invented here (the citations follow below); what is new is the object the rule monitors.

The primitive problem is entry timing under uncertainty (McDonald and Siegel, 1986; Dixit and Pindyck, 1994). A founder–institution pair — modeled as a single joint-value-maximizing agent, an abstraction from founder–TLO bargaining that SM-C flags — chooses a founding time $\tau$:
$$\sup_{\tau} \; \mathbb{E}\Big[\, e^{-r\tau}\big( G(\sigma_\tau, F_\tau, k_\tau) - I \big) \,\Big|\, \mathcal{F}_0 \Big] \qquad \text{s.t.} \quad dF_t = (R_{net} - B)\,dt,\;\; F_t \ge 0,$$
where $G$ is the expected discounted value of the entered venture (flow $P \cdot R \cdot S - B$, stopped at post-entry funds exhaustion), $B$ the burn rate, $R_{net}$ the pair's self-generated net revenue, $I$ the irreversible founding cost, discounted at the founding date, and $F = 0$ is absorbing: the financing constraint is the stock constraint $F_t \ge 0$ under the displayed funds law, not a flow inequality. The display finances $I$ externally at entry; the variant in which founding is paid *from the runway* ($F \mapsto F_\tau - I$, feasible only at $F_\tau \geq I$) is carried in parallel throughout SM-C, and the choice between the two is not bookkeeping — it reverses the sign of the runway comparative static (Proposition 2(iii)). The macro state $\sigma_{SU}$ reverts toward a regime-dependent alignment level across three regimes ($S_0$ dormant, $S_1$ warming, $S_2$ aligned) with switching intensities $\Pi$ (uniformized at a stated annual rate; SM-C fixes the units); the technology gate $g_{TRL}(t) \in \{0,1\}$ opens at a deterministic date set by the maturation program. Two accounting conventions keep the model honest. First, $S$ is non-financing survival (Section 3): funding death lives at the $F = 0$ barrier, so mortality is counted once. Second, pre-founding funds are *earmarked* — gap funds, fellowships, residence budgets are use-it-or-lose-it money restricted to this project's preparation — so waiting books no payoff flow, yet is not free: it burns the earmarked stock and shortens the option's life. In the deep-tech case $R_{net} < B$ the funds stock is a deterministic clock, and the founding problem is an option with an expiry, not a perpetual one. What happens as the clock runs out depends on who pays $I$, and the model prices both endings: under external financing the entered venture near the floor cannot repay its founding cost and the model forces *non-entry* — running out of runway kills the option rather than triggering a desperate founding; under runway-funded founding the last feasible moment prices founding as nearly free at the margin, and the rule collapses to "found iff the flow is positive" — the Marshallian deadline exercise, and the model's honest home for the folk intuition of racing the runway.

The value function $V(\sigma, F, k)$ satisfies a system of Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman variational inequalities coupled across regimes by $Q$. Because regime switching makes the operator non-smooth, existence and uniqueness are established in the viscosity framework for weakly coupled systems (Crandall, Ishii and Lions, 1992; Ishii and Koike, 1991; Pham, 2009), and the development is confined to SM-C — but one piece of it is economics, not technique: the absorbing barrier at $F = 0$ *is* the financing constraint, priced as forced exit, and the entry-time jump $F \mapsto F - I$ is what lets the model distinguish money that funds patience from money that funds the venture (Proposition 2 below). What matters here is that the problem is well-posed: a unique value function exists, and with it a well-defined continuation region in which waiting is strictly optimal.

**Theorem 4** (the GO operator; proof and named conditions in SM-C). *At the boundary of the continuation region, value-matching and smooth-pasting hold, and — under a single-crossing condition SC that SM-C proves when the regime layers share a common diffusion loading and otherwise states, defends, and monitors numerically, and a generic-transversality condition GEN with a checkable sufficient form — they determine a free-boundary curve $\theta_\sigma^*(k; F)$ per regime, such that the optimal founding policy is*
$$\mathrm{GO}(t, i) = \mathbb{1}\big[\sigma_{SU}(t) \geq \theta_\sigma^*(k_t; F_t)\big] \cdot g_{TRL}(t).$$
*The threshold varies continuously with the primitives where GEN holds; on the attainable range of the constructed index it is interior wherever the entry region is non-empty, and the boundary reading — "founding is never optimal in regime $k$ at that runway" — is a well-defined output of the rule, not a failure of it (SM-C exhibits computed parameterizations just outside the interior region where exactly this occurs).* When $R_{net} = B$ the funds stock freezes and the curve collapses to the classical per-regime constants of the stationary benchmark; SM-C keeps that benchmark separate and labels it as such. The folk checklist has become a first-order condition. One feature distinguishes this from the regime-switching entry models whose mathematics we inherit — regime-wise thresholds, generator comparative statics, and financing-state effects are the established terrain of Guo and Zhang (2004), Guo, Miao and Morellec (2005), Jobert and Rogers (2006), Hassett and Metcalf (1999) and Boyle and Guthrie (2003), and threshold endogeneity to project economics is that tradition's defining property, not our addition. The state being monitored is not a firm-level cash flow — nothing so observable exists before day zero — but the constructed, auditable macro state $\sigma_{SU}$ of Section 3, with the stopping rule welded to the measurement layer: every argument of $\theta_\sigma^*$ is either a rubric-disciplined observable or a declared calibration object, so the rule inherits the measurement system's audit trail, and is exactly as meaningful as that system — no more.

**Proposition 2** (comparative statics as policy channels; proofs, computed signs, and regions in SM-C). *Under the named conditions: (i) $\theta_\sigma^*(k;F)$ is non-increasing in $P$ — proved globally, computed monotone. (ii) It is increasing in $B$ on the stated region; the opposing waiting-cost channel is real and is exhibited in isolation (a disclosed sweep in which extra burn hits only the waiting stage lowers the bar), while the composite sign did not reverse anywhere on the disclosed family. (iii) The funds effect is genuinely two-signed, and the computed threshold curve settles where each sign rules: under external financing of $I$ the curve over runway is dip-shaped — forced non-entry at the floor, a falling band where post-entry slack recedes exit risk, then a rise toward the perpetual threshold as runway increasingly buys option life (the Boyle–Guthrie channel: liquidity-constrained pairs rationally found earlier); under runway-funded founding the slope is positive throughout and the deadline limit is Marshallian. The earlier uniform claim $\partial\theta^*/\partial F < 0$ is withdrawn; it holds on the thin-runway band only. (iv) Persistence is regime-specific, under the now-explicit proportional-rescaling scheme: durability of the aligned regime lowers the aligned bar and — the policy-relevant cross-effect, proved under SM-C's coupling package and confirmed numerically — lowers the warming regime's bar; warming-regime persistence computes negative (unproved); dormant-regime persistence *raises* its bar — making a bad regime stickier is a founding tax, not a subsidy. (v) $\theta_\sigma^*(S_0;F) > \theta_\sigma^*(S_1;F) > \theta_\sigma^*(S_2;F)$ under regime-ordered flows, at every computed runway level.* Each statement is an instrument mapping, and the mapping now names channels rather than slogans: a larger addressable market ($P \uparrow$) rationally lowers the tailwind bar; burn pressure ($B \uparrow$) raises it when it burdens the venture, and only lowers it when it burdens the wait alone; a cheaper founding transaction ($I \downarrow$) lowers it — a fifth lever the literature prices as the irreversibility premium; durability of the *aligned* regime is a founding subsidy obtained without spending a yen — it is credibility of the good state, not stickiness in general, that helps, and the model prices the difference. The funds lever is deliberately *two* levers with opposite signs, and Section 7 rewrites the instrument catalogue accordingly. Figure 3 reports all of this computed at fully disclosed synthetic parameter values, with the diagnostics (mesh stability, the McDonald–Siegel degenerate limit, an up-set check of the threshold geometry at every state, and the outside-region exhibits) recorded in SM-C.5.

The technology gate enters multiplicatively, and the factorization is a theorem *given an assumption we state, defend, and delimit in SM-C*: the maturation ramp is deterministic and exogenous — program-driven, not regime-responsive, not chosen, not failure-prone within the window (failures appear in the venture ledger as slower $R$ and worse $S$, where the survival accounting books them). Under that assumption the optimal policy separates as $\mathbb{1}[\sigma \geq \theta_\sigma^*(k;F)] \cdot g_{TRL}$, and the separation carries the section's sharpest practical lesson: *macro-ready but tech-not* and *tech-ready but macro-not* are distinct, non-compensable failure modes — a consequence of the exogeneity of maturation, and exactly the discipline the composite scores of Section 4 cannot enforce. What the assumption excludes is stated with equal clarity: regime-dependent or endogenous or failure-prone maturation would couple the gate to the threshold and add a waiting-to-learn motive (the compound-option logic of McGrath, 1997) that this model deliberately does not contain; the registered program audits scheduled against realized gate dates, so the assumption is falsifiable, not decorative. The model's decision vocabulary is completed by a trichotomy defined in SM-C: GO (gate open, funds feasible, $\sigma$ at or above the curve), WAIT (a live option not yet worth exercising — including gate-closed states), and NO_GO (the entry region is empty on the attainable state space: the option is worthless, with venture-ledger annihilation $P \to 0$ as the canonical case). Section 6's readings use exactly this vocabulary.

Waiting, in this framework, is not indecision; it is an option held and priced — priced, concretely, as the optimally stopped accumulation of the shortfall between the venture's flow payoff and interest on the founding cost (SM-C, Remark C.1). The continuation value $V - (G - I)$ measures what the pair *owns* by not founding yet, and "too early," the deep-tech graveyard's most common epitaph, acquires a precise meaning: founding while $\sigma_{SU} < \theta_\sigma^*(k;F)$, exercising an option whose time value still exceeds its intrinsic value. The field's folk wisdom is thereby quantified rather than dismissed — with the addition that patience itself has a funding requirement, which is what the earmarked pre-entry instruments of Section 7 exist to meet.

The institutional ledger, absent from the GO formula, acts on the problem as a parameter of the laws of motion — and only through the C3′ carriers that define Section 4's admissible class. SM-C makes this formal in the minimal necessary form (Lemma C.5): writing $e$ for the institutional profile summary, realization and survival responses $R(\sigma,k;e)$, $S(\sigma,k;e)$ and the exposure drift are non-decreasing in $e$, and the value of the pair's option is non-decreasing in $e$ while the founding bar $\theta_\sigma^*(k;F;e)$ is non-increasing in $e$ through the readiness, survival, and exposure carriers; through the funding carrier ($A_5$: gap funds raise $R_{net}$ and slow the clock) institutional strength buys *patience*, whose threshold effect is two-signed by Proposition 2(iii). A stronger nursery, in short, provably raises what the option is worth, and lowers the bar through every carrier except the one whose job is to fund waiting. What we do *not* claim as a theorem is the converse sufficiency — that nothing decision-relevant escapes the two-ledger report. Within the model class that holds by construction (C3′ is the class definition); as a claim about the world it is precisely channel completeness, registered as Conjecture C.1 in SM-C and submitted to the Hansen-$J$ test of Section 7. The two-ledger governance that Theorem 3 forces is, on present evidence, a complete design; whether the world agrees is what the registry is for.

A word on operating the rule, because "operable" must survive contact with real data. As constructed, $\sigma_{SU}$ is a bounded index built from smoothed, low-frequency series subject to revision, and the regime $k_t$ is a latent state: nobody observes "warming" directly. In practice the rule runs on a filtered reading — a smoothed $\sigma_{SU}$ and a retrospectively assigned or filtered regime — so its real-time output carries classification risk and revision lag, and threshold crossings near regime boundaries are the least reliable readings exactly when they matter most. The theory of stopping under such partial observation replaces state thresholds with belief thresholds (Décamps, Mariotti and Villeneuve, 2005); that extension is out of this paper's scope and is a declared Tier-B item. What this paper commits to is the disciplined version of the naive rule: the registry files GO/WAIT/NO_GO readings computed from the deposited series definitions and smoothing windows, time-stamped before outcomes, with the filtering convention part of the deposit — so the rule's real-time error is *measured* by the program rather than assumed away by the model.

A scope note maintains the paper's measurement discipline. Everything in this section conditions on the constructed state $\sigma_{SU}$; nothing in this paper estimates its process parameters, identifies regime dates, or claims predictive validation. Those are Tier-B tasks — pre-registered, out-of-sample, multiplicity-corrected — deferred to the registered program of Section 7. Here the model is calibrated and exercised; it is not yet tested. Saying so plainly is, we believe, part of what a measurement paper owes its readers.

## 6. Retrospective calibration: eight projects

We now put numbers — deliberately coarse ones — behind the framework. The data are the operating records of a single deep-tech venture studio working with Japanese universities and national laboratories between 2007 and 2026: eight projects whose founding decisions span that period — five with decisions taken and twenty-four-month outcomes complete, one recently founded (outcome right-censored), and two approaching scheduled decision points (readings reported, outcomes pending). Five disclosures govern everything that follows. The cases are *composites*: identifying details are altered and institutions appear as anonymized types, with fidelity maintained on the variables the model reads. The exercise is *retrospective calibration, not validation*: outcomes were known when the rubrics were scored, so agreement demonstrates that the model can organize known history, not that it predicts unknown futures — the registered, prospective test is Section 7's business. And the sample is *selected*: eight projects from one studio's pipeline carry survivorship and idiosyncrasy we do not pretend to correct with $N = 8$; the section's role is illustration under honest labels (hence the Tier-A vocabulary maintained throughout). The concordance accounting is *asymmetric by construction*: of the six decisions so far taken, four were taken by parties other than the studio — T, Y, and K, which the instrument reads, and Q, which it cannot — and the studio-involved cases (R, L, and the pending M and N) are nearly definitionally concordant, because the model formalizes the studio's own decision process, and a formalization that agrees with its source is not evidence; the effective externally-decided retrodiction sample is therefore four cases, of which three are readable, and the section should be cited at that size rather than as an unbroken concordance. And the timeline carries a *circularity we can bound but not remove*: the model was formalized in 2025–2026, so the decisions at T, Y, K, and Q — and the studio's early involvement at R — predate any version of it, while the most recent readings (L, M, N) were taken in an operating environment already informed by the model's precursors; concordance on those cases is evidence about the model-plus-studio bundle, not an independent test of the model.

Table 2 reports, per project: the $\sigma_{SU}$ regime at the decision point; the technology gate $g_{TRL}$; the slack state $F$; the model's reading ($\mathrm{GO}$ / $\mathrm{WAIT}$ / $\mathrm{NO\_GO}$); the decision actually taken; and a twenty-four-month outcome class (progressing / stagnant / terminated; right-censored where the decision is recent or pending). The classes carry a distinction the panel would otherwise blur: *progressing* requires documented milestone or revenue advance against the decision-date plan, and *stagnant* is survival without such advance, whatever pays for the survival. The funding source is not the classifier — what the funding purchased is: a competitive, milestone-gated public grant that buys maturation milestones is progress (Project R), while tailwind-funded persistence with no milestone movement is the class definition of stagnation (Project T, the zombie reading). Scores are reported at rubric level; the calibration constants mapping rubrics to model ranges are described as procedure (Section 3) and withheld as values, consistent with the proprietary-data declaration. Two agreements and one instructive tension organize the reading, and two projects deserve the space of a vignette.

The first vignette is the *zombie type*. Project T (materials; national-university host of type NU-A) sat in a domain whose macro alignment was, by any reading of the momenta, exceptional — construction-sector decarbonization policy, industrial pull, and academic output jointly holding the aligned regime $S_2$ at the decision date, as Table 2 records. The technology, however, had never left its originating laboratory: realization readiness at founding was near the rubric floor ($g_{TRL} = 0$ under any defensible gate). The model reads $\mathrm{WAIT}$ — a hold on the technology axis regardless of macro alignment, because the gate multiplies rather than averages. The historical decision was to found early on the strength of the tailwind; the outcome was a decade of stagnation kept alive by the very tailwind that motivated the founding — a zombie, in the field's own argot. The counterfactual is the section's point: *any* monotone composite of a venture score and its glowing macro-institutional environment would have let the environment compensate the missing technology — the compensation error whose portfolio-level scar Corollary 3.1 registers (a reversal is a property of samples, not of one decision). The two-ledger reading, by refusing the compensation, prices the case correctly ex post.

The second vignette is the *instant-fail type*. Project Y (energy; regional-university host of type RU-B) presents the mirror image: a technology gate that plausibly passes — prototypes existed, the maturation path was legible — attached to a potential-scale factor that unit-economics arithmetic drives to zero at physically attainable performance — a margin-viability zero in the sense of Section 3's $P$ construct, not a market-size zero. Multiplicative annihilation ($P \to 0 \Rightarrow \mathrm{PRS} \to 0$) reads the project as unfoundable at any macro state, which matches the eventual assessment after real resources had been spent discovering it; the inputs to that arithmetic — attainable performance and cost envelopes — were, on the records available to the scoring, knowable at the decision date, so what history purchased was the conclusion, not the data. The instructive comparison is with Project T: an additive or averaged score ranks Y *above* T — decent technology, decent institution, modest macro — precisely inverting the model's (and hindsight's) ordering. The pair of vignettes exhibits the two composite failure modes of Table 1 with live ammunition: addition ranks corpses; unguarded compensation founds zombies.

Across the eight cases read together (Table 2), the type-level pattern is consistent with the causal-channel structure — read, as SM-B.6 insists, as two different regressions rather than one. Under *decision-time stratification* (venture states fixed as of the decision date, the institution still to act on the remaining journey), projects hosted at higher-$\mathrm{ERS}$ types show faster realization drift and better survival; under *trajectory conditioning* (the realized trajectories themselves held fixed), $\mathrm{ERS}$ shows no residual association with outcomes — the zero C3′ requires once everything the institution transmits is recorded in the trajectory it was transmitted through. The section's instructive tension is a third, compositional pattern: in this funded panel the stronger host types tend to hold the earlier-stage, lower-$\mathrm{PRS}$ projects — perverse as a causal reading, and predicted as a selection reading, since a funded portfolio is a score-selected sample in which SM-B.6's selection lemma induces exactly this negative venture–institution dependence (the collider scar behind signature (i)). We state all three as descriptive readings of a small, selected panel, not as identified effects; the distinction is not modesty but method (Section 3's Tier discipline), and the identified versions are precisely what the registered program is built to test.

An accounting is owed of what the panel does not contain, and of what it contains that fits awkwardly. No discordant retrospective reading was omitted: the projects reported are the projects scored, and no case was dropped for disagreeing with its outcome. The awkward material is already in the table, and we point at it rather than around it. Project Q is a *reading failure of the instrument*: its decision-point record does not support rubric scoring at the evidence standard of Section 3, and for such a case the instrument returns "insufficient evidence" rather than a forced reading — a designed output class, and the honest price of an evidence-anchored rubric is that some historical cases cannot be read at all. Project R's GO was *marginal* in a stated sense: the decision-date reading sat near the boundary of the entry region, close enough to the threshold curve that modest perturbations of the calibration, or of a single rubric level, would return WAIT instead; we report GO because that is what the procedure returns, and we report the margin because a reader should know how little daylight the reading has. A panel whose most awkward contents are one unreadable case and one near-threshold call remains a panel scored by its own authors with outcomes known; those two defects are counted below.

The twenty-four-month window is defended here rather than footnoted. It is an *early-trajectory classifier*, not a terminal outcome: at twenty-four months a pre-founding decision has revealed the shape of the trajectory it produced — milestones moving, milestones frozen, or wound up — and that shape is all the class claims to read. Its known failure mode is the slow domain: in drug discovery or advanced materials, "stagnant at twenty-four months" can be the normal trajectory of a good project, so a domain-uniform window would systematically misclassify slow-good ventures in exactly the domains this paper claims for its scope. The registry therefore carries domain-specific outcome-class definitions — drug discovery flagged provisional, as Table 2's note already does for its readiness mapping — and the misclassification risk of slow-good ventures is registered as a known limitation with a stated mitigation: longer-horizon re-reads at forty-eight and sixty months as the registry matures, so that the early classifier is itself audited against the horizons its slowest domains run on.

The limitations paragraph is short because the design already stated most of it: $N = 8$, one studio, one national ecosystem, retrospective scoring, composite disclosure. Two of its defects deserve their own clause, because no amount of care removes them from a hindsight design: the retrospective panel is *single-rater* and *outcome-aware* — one scorer, who knew every completed outcome. These are the two design defects hindsight scoring cannot escape, and the registry protocol escapes them prospectively instead: two independent raters, blind to outcomes, with reliability reported — the requirement SM-B.6.5(2) already imposes for the registered diagnostics — and outcome classes adjudicated under the deposited definitions by a party other than the forecaster. What the section establishes is deliberately bounded — that the two-ledger system *can* be scored from records that actually exist before day zero, that its readings organize known outcomes without epicycles, and that the composite alternatives fail on these same records in exactly the modes the theory names. What it does not establish is prediction; for that, the model must put its thresholds on the record *before* outcomes arrive, which is the program to which we now turn.

## 7. Policy implications and a research program

For evaluators, the direct instruction is negative and liberating: retire the institution-adjusted single ranking, because Theorem 3 shows the object it approximates does not exist. What replaces it is not chaos but explicit structure. Where a one-dimensional shortlist is genuinely required, use the additive form $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$ *as a decision aid* — with a liveness precondition to guard its known failure mode, and with $(\alpha, \beta)$ published as the political trade-off they are rather than buried as calibration. Where structure permits, prefer conditional gating: rank ventures by the venture ledger within the population whose institutions clear stated thresholds. Both devices are honest because they carry their assumptions on the outside.

Neither device asks an agency to attempt something untried; the field already half-knows this. Horizon Europe's standard evaluation — three award criteria, Excellence, Impact, and Quality and efficiency of implementation, each scored 0–5 with a threshold of 3 per criterion and 10 overall [verify at S6] — is a gated-additive design, Table 1's fourth family: an additive decision aid with liveness guards by construction, fielded at the scale of the world's largest research and innovation program, and this section's conditional gating in deployed form. The United Kingdom's Knowledge Exchange Framework publishes perspective-level dashboards and deliberately declines to compress them into a single composite ranking [verify at S6]: a national funding body operating, in production, the refusal that Theorem 3 licenses — field evidence that two-ledger, no-merger governance is implementable at national scale. HEInnovate, the European Commission–OECD self-assessment framework for entrepreneurial higher-education institutions, scores eight dimensions, several of which overlap the axes of SM-D [verify at S6]; it is a self-diagnostic, not a measurement instrument: no aggregation theory licenses a total, no audited evidence standard disciplines the scoring, and nothing links the institutional profile to a venture-level object. The AUTM and ASTP benchmarking surveys, finally, observe activity metrics — disclosures, licenses, spin-outs — that sit downstream of the capabilities $\mathrm{ERS}$ scores [verify at S6]: complements to a capability rubric, not substitutes for one. Table 3 states the correspondence. What axiomatization adds to a HEInnovate-style self-assessment is four things: the aggregation license (Theorem 1: when the dimensions may be summed, and in what units), the gate (Proposition 1: which axis must not be), the audited missing-data discipline (E3; SM-A.5), and the causal linkage to a venture ledger (the C3′ carriers). What it borrows from KEF and Horizon Europe is the existence proof. Our devices, in short, explain and sharpen what the best committees already grope toward; the theorem tells them why their gated designs are right — and names what those designs still get wrong: merging the layers, imputing unknowns silently, leaving gates unanchored, and yielding, each budget cycle, to the ministry's demand for the composite.

For research institutions, the additive form is the point: $\mathrm{ERS}$ is a gap-diagnosis instrument, not a beauty contest. Its decomposability tells a technology-transfer office *which axis*, improved next, moves readiness most per unit of effort — and Proposition 1 names the binding constraint: capabilities behind a missing legal pathway are idle capacity. The gate is not, however, a rules-first sequencing instruction. In observed practice the first deals typically precede and produce the rules — the pathway is exercised into existence, then codified — and bylaws drafted by administrators in advance of any deal are themselves a known pathology, blocking the deals they were written to enable; what the gate demands is an *exercised* pathway (SM-D's level-2 anchor: core rules adopted and used at least once), not administratively pristine rules written before any deal. The sequencing Proposition 1 implies is iterated deal–rule co-evolution, with the gate scoring the co-evolution's residue rather than the order in which it happened. The temptation the ledger removes is the tournament reading, in which institutions optimize a rank rather than a bottleneck; a published national ranking of $\mathrm{ERS}$ would recreate at the institutional layer exactly the pathology Theorem 3 diagnoses at the merged layer.

One deployed instrument deserves a paragraph of its own, because the model prices it exactly and the price is negative. Programs that condition their grants on incorporation within the program period are negative timing instruments: they impose an artificial deadline on the founding option, and a deadline forces exercise at the boundary. In the model's terms, the rule at an imposed deadline collapses toward "found iff the flow is positive" — the Marshallian deadline exercise of SM-C's runway-funded convention — at whatever macro state happens to prevail: the instrument manufactures founding at $\sigma_{SU} < \theta_\sigma^*(k;F)$ by construction. This is how zombies are made at scale. The instrument pays for the premature GO; the tailwind that motivated the program then keeps the venture alive without milestone progress — the T-type of Section 6 — and the program's own success metric, incorporations per period, reads the damage as achievement. The repair belongs in the same breath, because it changes the deadline's object, not the program's generosity: deadline the *milestones*, not the incorporation — tranche the funds on maturation evidence and let the founding date float on the threshold rule — or run the deadline on the earmarked pre-entry money, where expiry prices the wait correctly, rather than on the founding event, where it forces the exercise. A program that counts incorporations buys incorporations; a program that wants ventures should count maturation.

The composite's persistence has a reason our replacement must answer, and it is not statistical naivety: the composite is an accountability artifact. A single published ranking hands the program officer an audit trail — a definite exhibit for the finance authority and the parliamentary question — while two ledgers plus deliberation reads, inside an agency, as more discretion, which is what agencies fear. The answer lives inside the paper's own apparatus: gates are more auditable than continuous coefficients. "We fund only ventures whose institutions clear stated, published thresholds" survives an audit better than any tuned weighting, because the audit question "why this cutoff?" has a stated answer — the anchor sheet and the precondition argument behind Proposition 1 — whereas "why this weight?" provably has none: Theorem 3 says the object such a weight would calibrate does not exist. Public weights on the additive decision aid, liveness guards, and institutional gates each carry their assumptions on the outside; that, and not the absence of judgment, is what audit-defensibility means.

For policy design, Proposition 2 converts slogans into channels — and the channels do not all point the same way, which is the point. Instruments that read as synonyms — "support deep-tech ventures" — act on *different arguments of the founding threshold*: subsidies and demand commitments raise $P$ and lower the bar; shared facilities and cost discipline lower $B$ and lower it again (when the burn burdens the venture rather than only the wait); standardized, low-cost founding procedures lower $I$ — a fifth lever, the irreversibility premium, cheap because it is administrative. Program credibility is a lever with a precise address: durability of the *aligned* regime lowers the founding bar both in the aligned regime and — the case that matters to a founder still in the warming regime — in the regime she may switch out of; making a *dormant* regime stickier, by contrast, raises its bar. Credibility of the good state is a founding subsidy without a yen of transfer; stickiness in general is not. One qualification keeps the lever honest: regime persistence is an equilibrium belief object, not a dial — founders discount announcements precisely because governments cannot bind their successors — so the lever is exercised through commitment devices, multi-year appropriations defended against single-year budget principles, statutory program structures, whose fiscal cost is the true price of the "free" subsidy; the model prices the effect of credibility, not the cost of manufacturing it. Funding instruments require the finest distinction, because the model prices two different monies. *Gap funds, bridging fellowships, laboratory residence* are earmarked pre-entry money: they extend the option's life, funding a viable project's $\mathrm{WAIT}$ — that is their function, and it operates the Boyle–Guthrie channel, buying patience rather than hastening founding. *Committed post-entry finance* — follow-on commitments, entry-time matching funds, a patient first customer — is the money that lowers the founding bar itself, and it dominates exactly where runway is the binding worry: the computed threshold curve of Figure 3 is dip-shaped in runway, falling where post-entry slack recedes exit risk and rising where additional runway mostly buys patience, so the same yen of "funding support" is a founding subsidy on one side of the dip and a patience subsidy on the other. A program that wants earlier founding should commit post-entry money; a program that wants better-timed founding should fund the wait; the earlier draft of this paragraph sold both effects as one derivative, and the model, taken seriously, refuses the conflation. Timing support is therefore not a rival of founding support but a different instrument with a different sign, and both are priced by the same threshold curve $\theta^*(k;F)$ reported in Figure 3.

One honesty about the catalogue completes it: the channels are clean, and real instruments are not — they load on several arguments at once, and the loading is a design variable, not a property of the instrument's name. A gap fund is slack and purchased maturation in one envelope: the same yen extends the option's life or accelerates $R$, and tranche design chooses the mix — milestone-gated tranches force the money to become realization rather than runway. A demand commitment raises $P$; a production subsidy repairs unit economics, which in Section 3's construct is also $P$; a "founding subsidy" may be any of the five levers wearing one name. The taxonomy's value is not that instruments map one-to-one onto channels — they do not — but that instrument design can choose its loading deliberately rather than inherit it by accident.

When the ministry nonetheless demands the composite ranking, the deliverable consistent with this section fits in three items, and a program officer can lift it as written. First, a venture shortlist ranked by $\mathrm{PRS}$ within the population whose institutions clear the published gates — $A_7 \geq L$, plus any program-specific axes. Second, where a single ordering is compulsory, the additive decision aid $\alpha \mathrm{PRS} + \beta \mathrm{ERS}$, liveness guard in force, with $(\alpha, \beta)$ published as the political trade-off they are. Third, the two-ledger budget split: project funding allocated by the venture ledger, capacity funding by the institutional ledger, and no line item that requires the number Theorem 3 forbids. The audit file answers "why these cutoffs?" with anchor sheets and "why no weights?" with a theorem — a better position than any composite has ever defended.

The research program — **Before Zero Studies** — is registered rather than promised. Its falsifiers are stated: the Corollary 3.1 diagnostics (pooled-sign reversal under score-selected sampling; within-stratum slope instability by cluster-robust Wald test) at a single family-wise error rate, with pre-registered power and equivalence bounds (SM-B.6); channel completeness (C3′) via GMM overidentification on institutional shocks; and, for the operating layer, a prospective registry in which the model's $\mathrm{GO}/\mathrm{WAIT}/\mathrm{NO\_GO}$ readings are filed *before* outcomes arrive, scored on twenty-four-month outcome classes, with a pre-committed discrimination floor (a Brier-score threshold) beneath which the model is declared to have failed (Popper, 1959; Lakatos, 1970). Protocols, rubrics, and outcome definitions are deposited on the Open Science Framework; the registry opens with the studio pipeline described in Section 6 and is built to admit other ecosystems.

The declared boundary bears repeating: deep-tech ventures, Japanese university and national-laboratory origins, the pre-founding stage. We regard portability — to other national systems, to software-dominant ventures, to corporate spin-outs — as the registry's question, not this paper's claim. What we do claim is that within its boundary the framework is complete: measurable with instruments that exist, impossible to collapse into the single score practice keeps demanding, and operable by a rule whose every parameter has an economic name.

## 8. Conclusion

Deep-tech ventures are largely decided before they legally exist, and the deciding stage has lacked a measurement theory. This paper built one: a venture ledger that multiplies because ventures die of their weakest link; an institutional ledger that adds — uniquely, by Theorem 1 — because institutions are portfolios of substitutable capabilities; a macro state that links them; and a proof (Theorem 3) that the two ledgers cannot be merged into any monotone single score once institutional capabilities act stage-contingently through the venture's dynamics. The go/wait decision, so often folklore, emerged as an optimal-stopping rule whose threshold is endogenous to project economics (Theorem 4), and eight retrospectively calibrated projects illustrated both the system's readings and the live failure modes of the composites it replaces.

Before day zero, value is real but unmeasured. The Before Zero Model makes it measurable — and the impossibility theorem stands guard over the practice, ensuring the two ledgers are not quietly re-merged into the convenient number that cannot exist. If the registered program falsifies the framework, the field will have learned something definite; if it survives, evaluation practice acquires foundations where it currently has habits. Either way, Before Zero Studies begins on an empirical footing rather than a rhetorical one.
