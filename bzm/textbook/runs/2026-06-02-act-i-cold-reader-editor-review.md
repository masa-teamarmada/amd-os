## Reader-first check

Company / author risk: low. The opening does not make AMD, Team ARMADA, the author, or a consulting method the hero. It follows a field situation and makes the subject "the phase before a company exists."

Reader self-recognition: high. University industry-collaboration staff, URA, young venture builders, researchers, support-program operators, and investors can all recognize their own room in the first sequence. The strongest reader hooks are:
- `00-prologue.md`: the too-strong deck sentence and the quiet researcher;
- `01-research-results-are-not-companies.md`: the missing budget owner after a positive corporate meeting;
- `02-different-clocks.md`: the same deck being pulled by support, research, IP, corporate, and investor clocks;
- `03-support-can-isolate-researchers.md`: the researcher being asked to stand forward and step back at the same time.

First-five-minute verdict: likely yes. A cold reader should feel "this is my field" before any formal theory appears.

Main weakness: the narrator sometimes tells the reader what the scene means too soon. The book is strongest when the reader watches the paper change; it is weaker when the narrator summarizes a taxonomy of what changed.

## Chapter-by-chapter notes

### 00 Prologue

Best part: lines 3-31 are a real opening. The deck sentence, applause, the sentence walking out of the room, and the researcher's silence give the book a genuine narrative engine.

Risk: lines 33-51 shift into definition. This is acceptable in a prologue, but line 43 is close to a disguised bullet list of the whole book's agenda. It works as an internal promise; it is less powerful as first-chapter prose.

Editor order for later surgical pass: keep the opening intact. Compress the `Before Zero` definition by making it grow from the deck artifact: what did the sentence know, what did it guess, what did it promise too early?

Chapter-end pull: strong. The final line makes the reader want to watch the sentence become "customer proof."

### 01 Research results are not companies

Best part: lines 27-51 are the cleanest reader-first sequence in Act I. The budget question, the silence, the revised sentence, and the researcher's small nod show the whole thesis without a framework.

Risk: lines 53-77 become explanatory in a more conventional business-book voice. They are true, but less alive than the meeting scene. Lines 79-95 preserve the author directive well, but they arrive as an essay insertion rather than a lived conflict.

Editor order for Act II-III: keep the J-curve / IPO critique as a protected directive, but make the next appearance happen inside a decision: a small paid pilot that either reveals a budget path or distracts from the real company path.

Chapter-end pull: strong. It explains why time pressure, not bad faith, creates the next mistake.

### 02 Different clocks

Best part: lines 3-31 and 35-61 are excellent Act I material. The same week contains the whole system: deadline, unpublished data, IP boundary, corporate evaluation, and CEO question.

Risk: lines 63-87 are the clearest residue of the old explanatory manuscript. The passage is not a markdown list, but it behaves like one: stakeholder by stakeholder, lesson by lesson. The later memo at lines 91-111 saves the chapter by returning the idea to an artifact.

Editor order for later surgical pass: shorten the `温度差ではなく、時計の差` section and let the five-clock memo do more work. The reader already understands the clock difference after the comments on the deck.

Chapter-end pull: strong. "時計のずれは、放っておくと孤独になる" is one of the best bridge lines in Act I.

### 03 Support can isolate researchers

Best part: lines 3-37 are a strong four-room sequence. The researcher is praised, restrained, pressed for conditions, and then asked who will run the company. This is exactly the right bridge to Act II.

Risk: lines 41-59 explain the support-menu problem in a list-heavy way. Lines 93-111 are useful setup for CEO-function decomposition, but they start sounding like a framework brief. Line 113, `Act Iで起きたことは`, should not remain in public manuscript body; it exposes the architecture label and breaks the illusion of reading a continuous book.

Editor order for later surgical pass: replace the `Act I` meta line with story language. For example, the public body should say "ここまでの一週間で起きたことは" or stay inside the case entirely.

Chapter-end pull: directionally strong. It clearly asks who carries what. It will become much stronger if Chapter 4 opens with a scene, not a CEO-function explanation.

## Act II rewrite orders

1. Open Chapter 4 in the same week, not in a definition. The researcher has just been praised for founder-like pitching, then hears "who will actually run this company?" Let that contradiction land before any CEO-function language.

2. Make CEO function a rescue from a false binary. The chapter must not start with "researcher CEO vs external CEO" as a framework. It should show the researcher hearing "外部CEO" as displacement, then reframe the question into functions only after the emotional risk is visible.

3. Make Chapter 5 a consequence of the traveling deck. Use the same overstrong sentence and revised customer-evidence line. The disclosure chapter should begin with a near accident: a phrase that works for a pitch but crosses a protected line in a university/IP review.

4. Make Chapter 6 the first real survival / earning decision. Do not write a generic "when to incorporate" essay. Put the team in a meeting where incorporation would satisfy support / investor clocks but would start obligations before budget owner, disclosure line, and operating owner are ready.

5. Stage the small-revenue tension as a real split. One paid signal should reveal customer pain, budget path, and repeatability. Another should tempt the team into low-ceiling work that narrows the company. This is where the J-curve / IPO critique becomes non-preachy.

6. Keep the artifacts alive. Act II should reuse:
- the too-strong deck sentence;
- the revised customer-evidence sentence;
- the five-clock memo;
- the `まだ誰も持っていないもの` field;
- a red/yellow/blue disclosure mark only if a character uses it in-scene.

7. Do not reintroduce workbook tables into Chapters 4-6. A visible table is allowed only if it is an artifact in the room and changes the next scene.

8. End each Act II chapter with an unresolved consequence. Chapter endings should not close with tidy lessons, checklists, or method summaries.
